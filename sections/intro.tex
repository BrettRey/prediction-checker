\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting told a committee of the House of Commons that the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission's (CRTC's) new rules for television would, \enquote{by 2020}, cost \enquote{some 15,130 media jobs}, \enquote{all of this as the direct result of Let's Talk TV regulatory changes} \citep[time marks 0900--0905]{commons2016chpc8}. The rules, the outcome of the CRTC's \textit{Let's Talk TV} proceeding, required cable, satellite and IPTV distributors to offer a \$25 entry-level package and to sell discretionary channels singly or in small bundles \citep{crtc2015worldofchoice}.

The number came from an economic-impact study by the consultancy Nordicity and Peter Miller, commissioned by Friends of Canadian Broadcasting and four other organizations in the sector; the report attributes its conclusions to the authors, not the commissioning groups \citep{nordicity2015television}. It modelled what the rules would do to distributors' and channels' revenue, to spending on Canadian programming, and, through input-output multipliers, to jobs and GDP through 2020, stating its assumptions link by link. CRTC series on revenue, programming spending and staff are now available for those years. My recorded searches of scholarly databases and the web found no published comparison of the forecast's numerical paths with outcomes (the queries are in the replication files). Miller's later work for the CRTC discusses what the decisions led to, and acknowledges an error in a separate prediction of station closures \citep[n.~88, n.~196]{miller2022rights}.

Economists have long compared what was predicted before a regulation with what happened after it. \textcite[ii]{harrington1999accuracy} found that ex ante estimates of direct costs exceeded actual costs for 12 of 25 environmental and occupational-safety rules and fell short for 6, with the errors in quantities \enquote{driven by both baseline and compliance issues}. A later set of case studies suggested \enquote{a slight tendency to overestimate both costs and benefits} \citep[285]{morgenstern2018retrospective}. \textcite{simpson2014overestimate} warns that a preponderance of overestimates need not show bias, since skewed cost distributions could produce one anyway; his review notes that the few studies of industry's own estimates found them above outcomes in most cases \citep[319--320]{simpson2014overestimate}.

The work I've cited concerns mainly regulators' estimates of compliance costs and benefits. This case is a different kind of forecast: an economy-wide estimate of jobs and output, made for stakeholder organizations and presented to legislators in testimony. In policy analysis generally, \enquote{Point predictions are common and expressions of uncertainty are rare} \citep[1]{manski2011certitude}.

A close precedent is \textcite{kane2026sffa}, who checked the admissions and enrolment forecasts made by universities and by Harvard's expert during the litigation that ended in the US Supreme Court's decision in \textit{Students for Fair Admissions v.\ Harvard}. He reports results against more than one baseline, and he separates the expert's technical estimate from the forecasts the institutions put to the Court. I make the same separation. The report's technical claim is a conditional forecast, which its authors offered as \enquote{the most plausible outlooks possible given available information} \citep[para.~iii]{nordicity2015television}: if the rules took effect and its assumptions held, revenue, programming spending and jobs would follow given paths. The public argument is what the committee was told.

I fixed the forecast record before downloading any CRTC outcome file, and ran a simulation, before opening those files, of whether they could tell a world with the forecast effect from one without it \citep{gelman2014types, gelman2020workflow}. The main outcome measure is a single number, \(k\): the observed shortfall below the report's own no-reform baseline over 2016--2019, the years before the pandemic, as a multiple of its forecast impact, so that \(k=0\) is the report's no-reform path and \(k=1\) its forecast.

The report's main input was uptake. Subscribers taking the entry-level package with a few chosen channels were to be \lttvByopSixteen{} of all subscribers in 2016, \lttvByopSeventeen{} in 2017 and \lttvByopEighteen{} from 2018, figures chosen from outside estimates of \lttvCitedUptakeLow{} to \lttvCitedUptakeHigh{}. The CRTC counted \lttvUptakeJuneShare{} at the end of June 2016, before every channel had to be offered singly as well as in small packages, and I found no later CRTC count. A survey by the Media Technology Monitor, released in August 2017, put adoption of starter packages at \enquote{just over 1 in 10 TV subscribers}, near the report's \lttvByopSeventeen{} for 2017 \citep{mtm2017starter}. Surveys aren't among the sources the analysis plan accepted for uptake, so the survey is reported beside the planned reading and the results below are given under both.

Specialty and pay channels' revenue, on which most of the job figure depends, stayed above the report's no-reform baseline: \(k=\lttvKSpec\) (standard error \lttvKSpecSE{}; 95\% interval \lttvKSpecLoTwo{} to \lttvKSpecHiTwo{}), an interval that treats the scale of the baseline's error as known. At the pre-stated error scale, that's inconsistent with the report's forecast path, though not at the top of the plan's range of error scales. What it says about the report's chain depends on how uptake developed after June 2016. Recalculated with uptake held at the CRTC's count and the exemption-order and closure dollar components fixed, the chain gives \(k=\lttvKPredSpecObsUptake\), compatible with the outcome at the designated error scale; on an illustrative survey path, 10\% from 2017, it gives \(k=\lttvKPredSpecSurvey\), \lttvKPredSpecSurveySEs{} standard errors above the estimate, though below the interval's upper end (\lttvKSpecHiWidest{}) at the widest calibration.

Distributors' revenue ran below the forecast path, and its interval (\(k=\lttvKBDU\), \lttvKBDULoTwo{} to \lttvKBDUHiTwo{}) includes the forecast. Split into subscribers and revenue per subscriber and measured from 2014, subscribers fell a further \lttvDecSubObs{} below the report's baseline path, against \lttvDecSubFc{} in its scenario with faster cord cutting, and revenue per subscriber rose only \lttvRevPerSubObsGrowth{} while the baseline path rose \lttvRevPerSubBaseGrowth{}. Of the total 2019 revenue shortfall below the report's baseline, in logs and measured from 2014, \lttvDecBeyondCounted{} lies beyond the recalculated chain with uptake held at the CRTC's count and \lttvDecBeyondSurvey{} on the survey path, where distributors' recalculated \(k\) rises from \lttvKPredBDUObsUptake{} to \lttvKPredBDUSurvey{} against the observed \lttvKBDU{}. Of the uptake paths tested, only one keeps both series compatible with the recalculated chain at the designated error scale: a rise from the 2016 count to the \lttvMorrisonUptake{} Morrison cited, by 2019. On the survey path the channel outcome isn't compatible, and with uptake held at the 2016 count the distributor outcome isn't, by a margin (\lttvKPredBDUObsUptakeSEs{} standard errors) that alternative calibrations remove. Streaming is one candidate for what the chain misses (Netflix's US paid memberships grew faster than the report's forecast of them); the paper doesn't test it.

Two descriptive comparisons need no assumption about later uptake. Per subscriber, distributors' payments to Canadian channels rose \lttvPerSubGrowth{} from 2015 to 2019, against \lttvBaseFeeGrowth{} in the report's no-reform fee path; under the decisions the report has those fees falling below that path as distributors pass on part of their losses. The two fee measures differ by definition, so only their growth compares. Staff counts didn't follow the revenue pattern the chain suggests: channels' staff fell by more than the forecast's direct FTE loss while their revenue stayed above the baseline, and distributors' staffing stayed near its pre-2015 trend while their revenue fell.

Morrison's summary to the committee departed from the report in three identifiable ways. It described an economy-wide estimate, more than half of it spin-off employment in other sectors, as \enquote{media jobs}. It described the report's 18\%, which the report's own figures don't reproduce, as a share of \enquote{what now exists} rather than of projected 2020 spending. And it didn't connect the job figure to the uptake assumption, or reconcile it with the lower uptake estimate he cited when asked. His appearance also presented the losses as open to policy, citing the authors' proposals to reduce them.
