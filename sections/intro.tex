\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting told a committee of the House of Commons that the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission's (CRTC's) new rules for television would, \enquote{by 2020}, cost \enquote{some 15,130 media jobs}, \enquote{all of this as the direct result of Let's Talk TV regulatory changes} \citep[time marks 0900--0905]{commons2016chpc8}. The rules, the outcome of the CRTC's \textit{Let's Talk TV} proceeding, required cable, satellite and IPTV distributors to offer a \$25 entry-level package and to sell discretionary channels singly or in small bundles \citep{crtc2015worldofchoice}.

The number came from an economic-impact study by the consultancy Nordicity and Peter Miller, commissioned by Friends of Canadian Broadcasting and four other organizations in the sector; the report attributes its conclusions to the authors, not the commissioning groups \citep{nordicity2015television}. It modelled what the rules would do to distributors' and channels' revenue, to spending on Canadian programming, and, through input-output multipliers, to jobs and GDP through 2020, stating its assumptions link by link. CRTC series on revenue, programming spending and staff are now available for those years. My recorded searches of scholarly databases and the web found no published comparison of the forecast's numerical paths with outcomes (the queries are in the replication files). Miller's later work for the CRTC discusses what the decisions led to, and acknowledges an error in a separate prediction of station closures \citep[n.~88, n.~196]{miller2022rights}.

This paper compares the report's intermediate predictions (the revenue of specialty and pay services and of distributors, uptake, payments, closures and staff) with what happened through 2019, and compares the report with what the committee was told. It tests neither the total employment forecast nor the decisions' causal effect. In the comparisons fixed in advance, at the error scales the plan designated, specialty and pay revenue, on which most of the job figure depends, stayed above the report's baseline and lost less ground against it than any uptake estimate the report cited implies; distributors' revenue was consistent with the forecast. The report's main input, the share of subscribers taking an entry-level package with chosen channels, was far above the CRTC's mid-2016 count of entry-level subscribers, though a 2017 survey, outside the sources the plan accepted, suggests starter-package adoption later came close to the report's figure. Among the uptake paths tested, at those scales, only a low one keeps both revenue series compatible with a partial recalculation of the report's chain. The testimony described the report's figures in two ways the report doesn't support and omitted the uptake assumption.

Economists have long compared what was predicted before a regulation with what happened after it. \textcite[ii]{harrington1999accuracy} found that ex ante estimates of direct costs exceeded actual costs for 12 of 25 environmental and occupational-safety rules and fell short for 6, with the errors in quantities \enquote{driven by both baseline and compliance issues}, and a later set of case studies suggested \enquote{a slight tendency to overestimate both costs and benefits} \citep[285]{morgenstern2018retrospective}. \textcite{simpson2014overestimate} warns that a preponderance of overestimates need not show bias, since skewed cost distributions could produce one anyway; his review notes that the few studies of industry's own estimates found them above outcomes in most cases \citep[319--320]{simpson2014overestimate}.

That work concerns mainly regulators' estimates of compliance costs and benefits. This case is a different kind of forecast: an economy-wide estimate of jobs and output, made for stakeholder organizations and presented to legislators in testimony. In policy analysis generally, \enquote{Point predictions are common and expressions of uncertainty are rare} \citep[1]{manski2011certitude}.

A close precedent is \textcite{kane2026sffa}, who checked the admissions and enrolment forecasts made by universities and by Harvard's expert during the litigation that ended in the US Supreme Court's decision in \textit{Students for Fair Admissions v.\ Harvard}. He separates the expert's technical estimate from the forecasts the institutions put to the Court, and I make the same separation. The report's technical claim is a conditional forecast, which its authors offered as \enquote{the most plausible outlooks possible given available information} \citep[para.~iii]{nordicity2015television}: if the rules took effect and its assumptions held, revenue, programming spending and jobs would follow given paths. The public argument is what the committee was told.

I fixed the forecast record before downloading any CRTC outcome file, and ran a simulation, before opening those files, of whether they could tell a world with the forecast effect from one without it \citep{gelman2014types, gelman2020workflow}. The main outcome measure is a forecast scale \(k\) for each revenue series, estimated over 2016--2019, the years before the pandemic: \(k=0\) corresponds to the report's baseline and \(k=1\) to its forecast, under a model that allows the baseline itself to be wrong. Analyses added after the outcome data was opened are labelled as such and listed in order in table~\ref{tab:chronology}.
