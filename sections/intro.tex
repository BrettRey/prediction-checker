\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting told a committee of the House of Commons that the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission's (CRTC's) new rules for television would, \enquote{by 2020}, cost \enquote{some 15,130 media jobs}, \enquote{all of this as the direct result of Let's Talk TV regulatory changes} \citep[time marks 0900--0905]{commons2016chpc8}. The rules, the outcome of the CRTC's \textit{Let's Talk TV} proceeding, required cable, satellite and IPTV distributors to offer a \$25 entry-level package and to sell discretionary channels singly or in small bundles \citep{crtc2015worldofchoice}.

The number came from an economic-impact study by the consultancy Nordicity and Peter Miller, commissioned by Friends of Canadian Broadcasting and four other organizations in the sector \citep{nordicity2015television}. It modelled what the rules would do to distributors' and channels' revenue, to spending on Canadian programming, and, through input-output multipliers, to jobs and GDP through 2020. Outcomes for those years are now public. I found no published check of the forecast in the scholarly databases and web sources I searched, or in the authors' later reports for the CRTC (the searches are listed in the replication files).

Economists have long compared what was predicted before a regulation with what happened after it. \textcite[ii]{harrington1999accuracy} found that ex ante estimates of direct costs exceeded actual costs for 12 of 25 environmental and occupational-safety rules and fell short for 6, with errors \enquote{driven by both baseline and compliance issues}. A later set of case studies found \enquote{a slight tendency to overestimate both costs and benefits} \citep[285]{morgenstern2018retrospective}. \textcite{simpson2014overestimate} warns that a preponderance of overestimates need not show bias, since skewed cost distributions could produce one anyway; his review notes that the few studies of industry's own estimates found them above outcomes in most cases \citep[319--320]{simpson2014overestimate}.

The work I've cited concerns mainly regulators' estimates of compliance costs and benefits. The forecasts that interested parties put to legislators are a different object. They predict economy-wide jobs and output through multipliers, they travel through testimony rather than regulatory filings, and I know of no routine for revisiting them. In policy analysis generally, \enquote{Point predictions are common and expressions of uncertainty are rare} \citep[4]{manski2011certitude}.

The closest precedent is \textcite{kane2026sffa}, who checked the enrolment forecasts made by universities and by Harvard's expert during the litigation that ended in the US Supreme Court's decision in \textit{Students for Fair Admissions v.\ Harvard}. He reports results against more than one baseline, and he separates the expert's technical estimate from the forecasts the institutions put to the Court. I make the same separation. The report's technical claim is a conditional projection: if the rules took effect and its assumptions held, revenue, programming spending and jobs would follow given paths. The public argument is what the committee was told would happen.

I fixed the forecast record before downloading any outcome data, and ran a simulation, before opening the data, of whether they could tell a world with the forecast effect from one without it \citep{gelman2014types, gelman2020workflow}. The main outcome measure is a single number, \(k\): the observed shortfall below the report's own no-reform baseline over 2016--2019, the years before the pandemic, as a multiple of its forecast impact, so that \(k=0\) is no effect and \(k=1\) is the forecast.

The largest error was in an input, not the model. The report assumed that subscribers taking the entry-level package with a few chosen channels would be \lttvByopSixteen{} of all subscribers in 2016 and \lttvByopEighteen{} from 2018, a figure it chose from outside estimates of \lttvCitedUptakeLow{} to \lttvCitedUptakeHigh{}. The CRTC counted \lttvUptakeJuneShare{} at the end of June 2016. Specialty and pay channels' revenue, on which most of the job figure depends, didn't fall: \(k=\lttvKSpec\) (95\% interval \lttvKSpecLoTwo{} to \lttvKSpecHiTwo{}).

At the pre-stated calibration of the baseline's error, that result is inconsistent with the report's forecast path at its assumed uptake. It's consistent with a model-implied scenario, the report's own chain run on the uptake the CRTC counted, which gives \(k=\lttvKPredSpecObsUptake\); consistent isn't confirmed, and the scenario assumes uptake stayed near its 2016 level. Distributors' revenue fell further than forecast (\(k=\lttvKBDU\)), and well beyond what the model gives at counted uptake (\(k=\lttvKPredBDUObsUptake\)), which points to losses from outside its unbundling channel. Streaming, which in the United States grew faster than the report forecast, is one candidate; the paper doesn't test it.

Two further checks don't depend on uptake. Per subscriber, distributors' payments to Canadian channels rose \lttvPerSubGrowth{} from 2015 to 2019, against \lttvBaseFeeGrowth{} in the report's own no-reform fee path, so the report's fee assumptions were too pessimistic before any pass-through could matter. And observed staffing doesn't track the model's revenue-to-jobs chain in either sector: channels shed more staff than the forecast's direct FTE loss while their revenue held, and distributors' staffing stayed near its pre-2015 trend while their revenue fell.

The public argument fared worst. The testimony gave a conditional projection as unconditional jobs lost, called an economy-wide estimate, more than half of it spin-off employment in other sectors, media jobs, and cited a share of programming spending that the report's own figures don't reproduce.
