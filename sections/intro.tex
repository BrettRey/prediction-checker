\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting told a committee of the House of Commons that the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission's (CRTC's) new rules for television would, \enquote{by 2020}, cost \enquote{some 15,130 media jobs}, \enquote{all of this as the direct result of Let's Talk TV regulatory changes} \citep[time marks 0900--0905]{commons2016chpc8}. The rules, the outcome of the CRTC's \textit{Let's Talk TV} proceeding, required cable, satellite and internet-protocol (IPTV) distributors to offer a \$25 entry-level package and to sell discretionary channels singly or in small bundles \citep{crtc2015worldofchoice}.

The number came from an economic-impact study by the consultancy Nordicity and Peter Miller \citeyearpar{nordicity2015television}, commissioned by Friends of Canadian Broadcasting and four other organizations in the sector; the report attributes its analysis and conclusions to the authors, not necessarily the commissioning groups. It modelled what the rules would do to distributors' and channels' revenue, to spending on Canadian programming, and, through input-output multipliers, to jobs and GDP through 2020, stating its assumptions link by link. CRTC series on revenue, programming spending and staff are now available for those years. My recorded searches of scholarly databases and the web found no published comparison of the forecast's numerical paths with outcomes (the queries are in the replication files). Miller's own later work for the CRTC \citeyearpar[n.~88, n.~196]{miller2022rights} discusses what the decisions led to, and acknowledges an error in a separate prediction of station closures.

This paper compares the report's revenue predictions for specialty and pay services and for distributors with outcomes through 2019, adds descriptive checks of uptake, payments, closures and staff counts, and compares the report with what the committee was told. It tests neither the total employment forecast nor the decisions' causal effect.

The report's main input is uptake, the share of subscribers taking an entry-level package with chosen channels. The main findings are these:
\begin{itemize}
\item In the comparisons the plan fixed in advance, allowing for error in the report's own baseline at the scale the plan designated for each series, specialty and pay revenue, on which most of the job figure depends, stayed above the report's baseline and lost less ground against it than the report's chain implies at any of the outside uptake estimates it cited (\lttvCitedUptakeLow{} to \lttvCitedUptakeHigh{}). Distributors' revenue was consistent with the forecast, though too imprecisely to tell a shortfall half the forecast's size from one twice as large. Under the largest error scales that alternative calibrations give, both verdicts become inconclusive; integrating over the error scale instead of fixing it, in checks added after the outcome data was opened, leaves both unchanged when the four years of outcomes also inform the scale, while with the scale set by a prior alone the result depends on the prior.
\item The report assumed \lttvByopSixteen{} uptake for 2016, far above the \lttvUptakeJuneShare{} of subscribers the CRTC counted on the entry-level package in mid-2016. A 2017 survey, outside the sources the analysis plan accepted, suggests starter-package adoption later came close to the report's \lttvByopSeventeen{} for 2017.
\item In partial recalculations of the report's chain from uptake to revenue, added after the outcome data was opened, only a low uptake path among those tested is compatible with both revenue estimates at the designated scales.
\item The testimony called an economy-wide employment estimate media jobs, presented the report's share of projected 2020 spending on Canadian programming as a share of current spending, and omitted the uptake assumption. The report's own totals put that share at \lttvCPEShareTwenty{}, not its stated 18\%.
\end{itemize}

Economists have long compared what was predicted before a regulation with what happened after it. \textcite[297]{harrington2000accuracy} found that ex ante estimates of total direct costs exceeded actual costs for 14 of 28 environmental and occupational-safety rules and fell short for 3, with the quantity errors \enquote{driven both by difficulties in determining the baseline and by incomplete compliance}, and a later set of case studies suggested \enquote{a slight tendency to overestimate both costs and benefits} \citep[285]{morgenstern2018retrospective}. \textcite[322]{simpson2014overestimate} warns that a preponderance of overestimates need not show bias, since skewed cost distributions could produce one anyway; his review notes that the few studies of industry's own estimates found them above outcomes in most cases (pp.~319--321).

That work concerns mainly regulators' estimates of compliance costs and benefits. This case is a different kind of forecast: an economy-wide estimate of jobs and output, made for stakeholder organizations and presented to legislators in testimony. Such estimates have a critical literature of their own. \textcite[67]{crompton2006impact} documents how the job and output totals of commissioned economic-impact studies are inflated through multipliers and generous assumptions about which spending is new, and independent studies of sports facilities have found no statistically significant positive association between facility construction and local development \citep[103]{siegfried2000sports}. This paper doesn't test the report's multipliers; it checks the predictions they were applied to. In policy analysis generally, \enquote{Point predictions are common and expressions of uncertainty are rare} \citep[1]{manski2011certitude}.

A close precedent is \textcite{kane2026sffa}, who checked the admissions and enrolment forecasts made by universities and by Harvard's expert during the litigation that ended in the US Supreme Court's decision in \textit{Students for Fair Admissions v.\ Harvard}. He separates the expert's technical estimate from the forecasts the institutions put to the Court, and I make the same separation. The report's technical claim is a conditional forecast, which its authors offered as \enquote{the most plausible outlooks possible given available information} \citep[para.~iii]{nordicity2015television}: if the rules took effect and its assumptions held, revenue, programming spending and jobs would follow given paths. The public argument is what the committee was told.

For the technical claim, I fixed the forecast record before downloading any CRTC outcome file, and ran a simulation, before opening those files, of whether they could tell a world with the forecast effect from one without it \citep{gelman2014types, gelman2020workflow}. The main outcome measure is a forecast scale \(k\) for each revenue series, estimated over 2016--2019, the years before the pandemic: \(k=0\) corresponds to the report's baseline and \(k=1\) to its forecast, under a model that allows the baseline itself to be wrong. Analyses added after the outcome data was opened are labelled as such and listed in order in table~\ref{tab:chronology}.
