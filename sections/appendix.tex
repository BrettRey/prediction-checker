\section{Estimation details}
\label{app:methods}

\subsection*{The outcome measure}
Take a revenue series with report baseline \(B_t\), forecast shortfall in dollars \(I_t\) (so the reform path is \(B_t-I_t\)) and observed value \(Y_t\) for \(t=2016,\dots,2019\). Let \(y_t=\log B_t-\log Y_t\) (the observed log shortfall below the baseline, negative when the outcome is above it) and \(\delta_t=\log B_t-\log(B_t-I_t)\) (the forecast log shortfall). The model, in which \(k\) scales the report's path of forecast shortfalls, is
\[
y_t = k\,\delta_t + e_t,\qquad e_t=\sum_{s=2015}^{t}\varepsilon_s,\quad \varepsilon_s\sim N(0,\sigma^2)\ \text{independent},
\]
so the outcome without the decisions would have been \(B_te^{-e_t}\), and the baseline's error \(e_t\) is positive when the baseline overstates it. The error is a random walk from 2014, the last actual year in the report, and
\[
\operatorname{Cov}(e_t,e_u)=\sigma^2\min(t-2014,\,u-2014).
\]
With \(\Sigma\) that covariance matrix, \(\hat k=\delta'\Sigma^{-1}y/\delta'\Sigma^{-1}\delta\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat k)=1/\delta'\Sigma^{-1}\delta\). The annual error scale \(\sigma\) is set by calibration rather than estimated from four observations, so the 95\% interval is \(\hat k\pm1.96\) standard errors with no degrees-of-freedom correction; it covers uncertainty about how far the baseline would have drifted, not uncertainty in measuring the outcomes, which are administrative totals, and not uncertainty in \(\sigma\) itself. As an illustration of the last, if the historical volatility were an estimate from four independent, normal annual changes, an interval using the \(t\) distribution with three degrees of freedom would be about \lttvTThreeWiden{} wider (specialty and pay \lttvKSpecTThreeLo{} to \lttvKSpecTThreeHi{}, distributors \lttvKBDUTThreeLo{} to \lttvKBDUTThreeHi{}); even that doesn't carry the uncertainty of the actual calibration rule. A partial recalculation's scenario coefficient is \(k^{u}=\delta'\Sigma^{-1}\delta^{u}/\delta'\Sigma^{-1}\delta\), where \(\delta^{u}\) is the path of log shortfalls recomputed at a given uptake path with the dollar components for the exemption order and closures held fixed; it's the value \(\hat k\) would take if \(y=\delta^{u}\). Table~\ref{tab:inputs} gives \(B_t\), \(I_t\) and its components, \(Y_t\), \(\delta_t\) and \(y_t\) for both series, and tables~\ref{tab:scenarios} and~\ref{tab:calibrations} the uptake paths and the values of \(\sigma\). The report's paths and the CRTC series are both in nominal dollars; since \(k\) compares them year by year in logs, deflating both by the same price index leaves it unchanged.

\subsection*{The cited-input band}
To construct the band, I project onto \(\delta\), in the same way, two paths built from the outside uptake estimates the report cites. In every year the unbundling and preponderance components are scaled by the ratio of Corus's lower estimate (\lttvCitedUptakeLow{}) or Oliver Wyman's (\lttvCitedUptakeHigh{}) to the report's \lttvByopEighteen{}, which keeps the shape of the report's uptake path; in the high case closures are also multiplied by \lttvCitedClosuresMult{}, for the higher closure estimates of Bell and Oliver Wyman \citep[paras.~201, 230]{nordicity2015television}. Table~\ref{tab:inputs} gives the band for each series.

\subsection*{Design analysis}
Before any CRTC outcome file was opened, I simulated each quantity 20,000 times (seed 20261003) in two worlds: without the forecast effect, \(Y_t=B_te^{-e_t}\), so that \(y_t=e_t\) and \(k=0\); and with it, \(Y_t=B_te^{-e_t}-I_t\), the forecast impact applied in levels to the drifted baseline. The second gives \(y_t=\delta_t+e_t\) (\(k=1\)) up to a term of order \(e_tI_t/(B_t-I_t)\). The simulation code writes the drift with the opposite sign, \(B_te^{e_t}\); since the errors are symmetric about zero, the two describe the same distribution. The extra term matters for coverage, because it scales the error in \(y_t\) by about \(B_t/(B_t-I_t)\): applied to paths simulated this way, the 95\% interval covers \(k=1\) in \lttvCoverDollarsSpec{} of them for specialty and pay revenue and \lttvCoverDollarsBDU{} for distributors' revenue, against 95\% when the impact applies proportionally, \(Y_t=(B_t-I_t)e^{-e_t}\) (\texttt{scripts/model\_check\_lttv.py}, seed 20261003); on that basis the specialty and pay estimate lies about \lttvKSpecSEsFromOneDollars{} simulated standard deviations from \(k=1\), against \lttvKSpecSEsFromOne{} standard errors. The estimation model is the proportional one, and the intervals in the text are valid under it, given \(\sigma\). Each simulated path was assigned to whichever of the report's two paths gives it the higher Gaussian likelihood under \(\Sigma\). \enquote{Classified correctly} is the share of paths assigned to the world that generated them, taking the smaller of the two worlds' shares. With the annual error scale set to the historical volatility of the report's own 2010--2014 data, the simulation classified specialty and pay revenue correctly in about \lttvDesignCorrectSpec{} of simulated worlds and distributors' revenue in about \lttvDesignCorrectBDU{}; CPE, whose historical volatility was \lttvHistSDCPE{} a year, came out at \lttvDesignCorrectCPE{}, so it was set aside in advance as descriptive.

\subsection*{Calibrating the annual error scale}
The report checked its forecast of Canadian streaming penetration against a US forecast built from Trefis's projection of US Netflix subscribers and Digital TV Research's of all subscription streaming \citep[paras.~135--137, pp.~50--51]{nordicity2015television}. So the error in the Netflix part of that US forecast is evidence about the technology side of the report's baseline, assumed unaffected by Canadian regulation; the calibration uses the Netflix row of Table~7, whose outcomes Netflix itself reports. The pre-stated rule sets \(\sigma\) to the larger of the series' 2010--2014 volatility and
\[
|\log(\text{observed}/\text{forecast})|/\sqrt{h}
\]
for US Netflix subscribers in 2018 (\(h=4\) years from 2014). For 2018 the report forecast \lttvNetflixFcEighteen{} million and Netflix reported \lttvNetflixObsEighteen{} million \citep{netflix2019form10k}, which gives \lttvSigmaTech{} a year, below the historical volatility of both revenue series, so the historical volatility sets \(\sigma\). The other calibrations apply the same formula to 2016 and 2017, pool 2016--2018 as a random walk, or measure the error in growth from 2015, where the report's starting figure already differed from Netflix's count. With \(n_t=\log(\text{observed}_t/\text{forecast}_t)\) for the Netflix series (a different quantity from the baseline's error \(e_t\)), the pooled value is
\[
\sqrt{\bigl(n_{2016}^2/2+(n_{2017}-n_{2016})^2+(n_{2018}-n_{2017})^2\bigr)/3},
\]
the maximum-likelihood standard deviation of a random walk from 2014 observed at \(h=2,3,4\); the growth-based values replace \(n_t\) with \(n_t-n_{2015}\) and count \(h\) from 2015. The Netflix-derived value applies only when it exceeds the series' historical volatility.

\subsection*{Integrating over the error scale (post hoc)}

The priors and what to report were fixed in \texttt{DECISIONS.md} before the first version below was computed. With a flat prior on \(k\) and \(\Omega\) the matrix with entries \(\min(h_s,h_t)\), integrating \(k\) out of the likelihood leaves
\[
p(y\mid\sigma)\propto\sigma^{-3}\operatorname{exp}\bigl(-\mathrm{RSS}/2\sigma^2\bigr),\qquad \mathrm{RSS}=y'\Omega^{-1}y-(\delta'\Omega^{-1}y)^2/\delta'\Omega^{-1}\delta,
\]
where \(\mathrm{RSS}\) is the residual sum of squares, and given \(\sigma\), \(k\) is normal with mean \(\hat k\) and variance \(\sigma^2/\delta'\Omega^{-1}\delta\) \citep[372]{GelmanBDA3}. The first prior on \(\sigma\) is uniform on the plan's sensitivity range, 1\% to 8\% a year. The second treats the report's 2010--2014 volatility \(s\) as an estimate from four annual changes: \(\sigma^2\) is scaled-inverse-\(\chi^2\) with 3 degrees of freedom and scale \(s^2\). In the version specified first, \(\sigma\)'s posterior combines its prior with \(p(y\mid\sigma)\); in the prior-only version, computed after it, the likelihood term is dropped, so the outcomes don't update \(\sigma\) and its prior weights the mixture (a cut posterior, not the ordinary posterior under this model). In both, the distribution for \(k\) is a mixture of normals over \(\sigma\), computed on a grid from 0.1\% to 20\% a year in steps of 0.01 points and checked against 400,000 Monte Carlo draws. Extending the grid to 100\% a year moves no 95\% end by more than \lttvKIntGridShiftMax{}; the largest move is in the prior-only distributors' interval under the history-based prior, whose lower end, \lttvKIntBDUHistOnlyLoThree{} (0.00 in table~\ref{tab:calibrations}), becomes \lttvKIntBDUHistOnlyLoWide{}. In the version specified first, the 50\% intervals are \lttvKIntSpecUnifLoFifty{} to \lttvKIntSpecUnifHiFifty{} (specialty and pay) and \lttvKIntBDUUnifLoFifty{} to \lttvKIntBDUUnifHiFifty{} (distributors) under the uniform prior, and \lttvKIntSpecHistLoFifty{} to \lttvKIntSpecHistHiFifty{} and \lttvKIntBDUHistLoFifty{} to \lttvKIntBDUHistHiFifty{} with the volatility as data. The band doesn't depend on \(\sigma\). Produced by \texttt{scripts/sigma\_integrated\_lttv.py}; data in \texttt{data/derived/sigma\_integrated\_lttv.csv}.
