\section{What the case shows}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read as a conditional forecast, the report raises three questions with different answers. Whether its main input failed depends on how uptake developed after June 2016 and how well the available measures match the report's: the CRTC's count then ran far below the report's 2016 assumption, and a 2017 survey, outside the plan's accepted sources, put uptake near its figure for 2017. Whether its mechanism worked depends on the same question, and the comparisons here can say only which uptake paths the outcomes are compatible with. With uptake held at the 2016 count, channel revenue is compatible with the recalculated chain, and distributors' revenue falls below it by \lttvKPredBDUObsUptakeSEs{} standard errors, a gap that alternative calibrations remove; the split shows that shortfall coming through both fewer subscribers and revenue per subscriber that grew far less than the baseline assumed. On the survey path, distributors' revenue comes closer to the chain, and channel revenue sits \lttvKPredSpecSurveySEs{} standard errors above what the chain projects, while payments to Canadian channels per subscriber grew faster than the report's no-reform fee path.

Of the paths tested, only a rise to about \lttvMorrisonUptake{} by 2019 keeps both series compatible with the recalculated chain, so the outcomes fit the report's mechanism only if uptake stayed well below its assumption, and the official counts that could settle the question stop in 2016. If the 2016 count was the miss, it's the kind of quantity error \textcite[ii]{harrington1999accuracy} found behind misses in regulatory cost estimates.

The jobs figure put to Parliament rests on that chain, and the report divides it two ways (table~\ref{tab:jobs-reconciliation}; \citealp[paras.~246, 248, 250; Tables~22--23, pp.~90--92]{nordicity2015television}). By kind of effect, \lttvJobsDirect{} of the \lttvJobsTotal{} FTEs are direct jobs in broadcasting and production and \lttvJobsSpinoff{} are spin-off jobs in other sectors. By the sector whose losses give rise to them, \lttvFTEProd{} are attributed to independent production (\lttvFTEProdDirect{} direct, \lttvFTEProdSpin{} spin-off) and \lttvFTEBroadcast{} to broadcasting (\lttvFTEBroadcastDirect{} direct, \lttvFTEBroadcastSpin{} spin-off). The \lttvFTESpecProd{} (\lttvFTESpecProdShare{}) attributed to specialty and pay services (\lttvFTESpec{}) and to production depend in the model on Canadian channels' revenue and programming spending.

The staff counts don't move with that chain: channels shed more staff than the forecast's direct FTE loss although their revenue stayed above the baseline, and distributors' staffing stayed near trend although their revenue fell. These are descriptive comparisons of unlike measures, which can't test the report's employment mechanism. This paper doesn't estimate the spin-off employment, and production employment isn't checked here.

Morrison's summary to the committee departed from the report in three identifiable respects. It described an economy-wide employment estimate, more than half of it spin-off employment, as \enquote{media jobs}; it described the report's 18\%, which the report's own figures don't reproduce, as a share of current spending; and it didn't connect the job figure to the report's uptake assumption, or reconcile it with the \lttvMorrisonUptake{} estimate he cited in questioning, a level at which the recalculated chain gives a projected \(k\) of \lttvKPredSpecMorrison{} (his figure, like the CRTC's count and the survey, refers to the entry-level package itself, not quite the report's measure, and his \enquote{4\% of Canadians} doesn't specify a denominator; the scenarios treat it as 4\% of subscribers). His appearance also presented the losses as open to policy, citing the authors' proposals to reduce them, and when a member raised officials' more reassuring view he answered, \enquote{If it is right, that's good news} \citep[time marks 0915--0920]{commons2016chpc8}. What was lost between report and committee was the numerical condition on which the headline rested, a particular case of the point \textcite[1]{manski2011certitude} makes about policy analysis generally, where point predictions are common and statements of uncertainty rare. These are limits of the presentation; the paper doesn't test the total employment effect.

The report deserves credit for what made this check possible. It stated its assumptions link by link, published annual paths for both scenarios, cited the range of outside estimates it chose among, gave the figures behind its headline, and said plainly that \enquote{there is no easy way to assign probabilities to the projections underlying future scenarios} \citep[para.~111]{nordicity2015television}. It raised the possibility of low uptake itself. Its commissioning organizations made that disclosure possible. Publishing only the headline would have left these intermediate predictions unavailable for checking, and the useful lesson for those who commission or hear such forecasts is in that contrast. Intermediate predictions that public data can test, an uptake share or a payment flow in the second year, let a forecast be monitored before its end date, provided someone counts them: official counts of uptake confined to 2016 are why this paper has to give two readings. And a statement of how wrong the baseline could be shows in advance how much the headline depends on it.

\subsection{Limits of the check}

The paper checks one forecast, and nothing here shows that stakeholder forecasts in general are biased; \textcite{simpson2014overestimate} explains why even a run of overestimates needn't show that. The verdict on channel revenue rests on how wrong the report's technology-only baseline is taken to be. I model that error as a random walk calibrated in several ways, a simple model for stating the uncertainty, and a structural break in viewing could exceed it. The intervals also treat the scale of that error as known, and they assume the forecast impact applies proportionally to the drifted baseline; if it applied in dollars, as in the design simulation, they'd cover the forecast in about \lttvCoverDollarsSpec{} of cases for channels and \lttvCoverDollarsBDU{} for distributors rather than 95\%, and the channel estimate would lie about \lttvKSpecSEsFromOneDollars{} simulated standard deviations from the forecast rather than \lttvKSpecSEsFromOne{} standard errors. The uptake evidence is thin: two CRTC counts from the spring and summer of 2016 and one self-reported survey from 2017, which may not measure what the report meant by uptake, so the uptake paths here illustrate the possibilities rather than bound them. The financial summaries used here can't separate closures of independent channels from moves to exemption, the aggregates can't identify the pass-through rate, and the staff counts are a different measure from the report's modelled employment. At its historical volatility, the design analysis gave Canadian programming expenditure too little power for the planned test.

Five findings don't depend on the model of the baseline's error: uptake in mid-2016 fell far short of the report's 2016 assumption; distributors' subscriber numbers fell faster than in either of the report's paths, if the two subscriber series stay comparable; payments to Canadian channels per subscriber grew faster than the report's own no-reform fee path; the CRTC's count of specialty and pay revenue for 2015, a year with no forecast impact, was already \lttvGapFifteenSpec{} \lttvGapFifteenSpecDir{} the report's figure, against \lttvGapFourteenSpec{} in 2014; and the testimony described the report's figures in two ways the report doesn't support and omitted the uptake assumption.

\section*{Data and code}

Every number in this paper can be reproduced from the repository at \url{https://github.com/BrettRey/prediction-checker}, which holds a script that downloads every public source used, the frozen prediction record, the scripts that read the report's figures and check them against its text, the design analysis, and the outcome analysis, with the CRTC series used (Open Government Licence~-- Canada). The analysis plan and each choice made after the outcome data was opened are recorded with dates in the repository. The case was first suggested in a list of candidate cases produced by ChatGPT (the latest model available on 3 October 2026, at the Pro effort level), which also proposed separating the report's technical claim from the public argument. It supplied leads and reported forecast figures, every one of which was checked against primary sources before use, and none of the calculations reported here. The analysis scripts were written with Claude Opus 5.5 and checked against the sources and against the report's printed totals.
