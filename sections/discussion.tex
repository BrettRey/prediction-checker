\section{What the case shows}
\label{sec:discussion}

Read as a conditional projection, the report did better than its headline. Its clearest miss was an input, the share of subscribers who would take the entry-level package, the kind of quantity error \textcite[ii]{harrington1999accuracy} found behind misses in regulatory cost estimates. Whether its mechanism worked can be answered only in part. Run at the uptake counted, as a model-implied scenario, its prediction for channel revenue is consistent with the outcome at the pre-stated calibration, which falls short of confirming it, and the channel data can't say more. For distributors the split of revenue gives a sharper answer: their loss came through both fewer subscribers and stagnant revenue per subscriber, each well beyond what the report's chain produces at the uptake that occurred. The fees they paid Canadian channels per subscriber ended above even the report's no-reform path.

The jobs figure put to Parliament rests on that chain, and the report divides it two ways (table~\ref{tab:jobs-reconciliation}; \citealp[paras.~246, 248, 250; Tables~22--23, pp.~90--92]{nordicity2015television}). By kind of effect, \lttvJobsDirect{} of the \lttvJobsTotal{} FTEs are direct jobs in broadcasting and production and \lttvJobsSpinoff{} are spin-off jobs in other sectors. By the sector whose losses give rise to them, \lttvFTEProd{} are attributed to independent production (\lttvFTEProdDirect{} direct, \lttvFTEProdSpin{} spin-off) and \lttvFTEBroadcast{} to broadcasting (\lttvFTEBroadcastDirect{} direct, \lttvFTEBroadcastSpin{} spin-off). The \lttvFTESpecProd{} (\lttvFTESpecProdShare{}) attributed to specialty and pay services (\lttvFTESpec{}) and to production depend in the model on Canadian channels' revenue and programming spending.

The staff counts don't move with that chain: channels shed more staff than the forecast's direct FTE loss although their revenue held, and distributors' staffing stayed near trend although their revenue fell. These are descriptive comparisons of unlike measures, which can't test the model's employment mechanism. Spin-off employment has no observable counterpart, and production employment remains to be checked.

The public argument is where the forecast failed outright. The testimony presented an economy-wide estimate, more than half of it spin-off employment, as media jobs that would be lost, as the direct result of the policy, and it quoted a share of programming spending that the report's own figures don't support. It also didn't say that the figures rested on an uptake assumption. In the same appearance Morrison estimated uptake at \lttvMorrisonUptake{}, a level at which the report's chain gives a projected \(k\) of \lttvKPredSpecMorrison{} (his figure, like the CRTC's counts, refers to the entry-level package itself, not quite the report's measure). On the way from report to committee, a conditional projection with stated assumptions became a point prediction without them. It's a particular case of the point \textcite[4]{manski2011certitude} makes about policy analysis generally, where point predictions are common and statements of uncertainty rare.

The report deserves credit for what made this check possible. It stated its assumptions link by link, published annual paths for both scenarios, cited the range of outside estimates it chose among, and gave the figures behind its headline. A forecast of a single number would have left nothing to check but the number. The useful lesson for those who commission or hear such forecasts is in that contrast. Intermediate predictions that public data can test, an uptake share or a payment flow in the second year, let a forecast be held to account before its end date, and a statement of how wrong the baseline could be shows in advance how much the headline depends on it.

\subsection{Limits of the check}

The paper checks one forecast, and nothing here shows that stakeholder forecasts in general are biased; \textcite{simpson2014overestimate} explains why even a run of overestimates needn't show that. The verdict on channel revenue rests on how wrong the report's technology-only baseline is taken to be. I model that error as a random walk calibrated in several ways, the simplest model that lets the uncertainty be stated, and a structural break in viewing could exceed it. The intervals also treat the scale of that error as known, and they assume the forecast impact applies proportionally to the drifted baseline; if it applied in dollars, as in the design simulation, they'd cover the forecast in about \lttvCoverDollarsSpec{} of cases for channels and \lttvCoverDollarsBDU{} for distributors rather than 95\%, and the channel estimate would be \lttvKSpecSEsFromOneDollars{} standard errors from the forecast rather than \lttvKSpecSEsFromOne{}. Entry-level uptake is known only for 2016, so the conditional reading assumes it stayed near that level; if it rose toward the report's assumption, that reading weakens. Closures of independent channels can't be separated from moves to exemption, the aggregates can't identify the pass-through rate, and the staff counts are a different measure from the report's modelled employment. Canadian programming expenditure moved too much from year to year, before the reforms, to test the forecast on that measure.

Five findings don't depend on the model of the baseline's error at all: uptake fell far short of the report's assumption; distributors' subscriber numbers fell faster than the report forecast in either of its scenarios; payments to Canadian channels per subscriber ended above the report's own no-reform path; the CRTC's count of specialty and pay revenue for 2015, a year with no forecast impact, was already \lttvGapFifteenSpec{} \lttvGapFifteenSpecDir{} the report's figure, against \lttvGapFourteenSpec{} in 2014; and the testimony stated more than the report did.

\section*{Data and code}

Every number in this paper can be reproduced from the repository at \url{https://github.com/BrettRey/prediction-checker}, which holds a script that downloads every public source used, the frozen prediction record, the scripts that read the report's figures and check them against its text, the design analysis, and the outcome analysis, with the CRTC series used (Open Government Licence~-- Canada). The analysis plan and each choice made after the outcome data was opened are recorded with dates in the repository. The case was first suggested in a list of candidate cases produced by ChatGPT (the latest model available on 3 October 2026, at the Pro effort level), which also proposed separating the report's technical claim from the public argument; it supplied no data and ran no analysis, and every claim taken from its list was checked against primary sources before use. The analysis scripts were written with Claude Opus 5.5 and checked against the sources and against the report's printed totals.
