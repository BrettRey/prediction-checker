\section{What the revenue evidence establishes and what the testimony omitted}
\label{sec:discussion}

In the pre-stated comparisons, specialty and pay revenue, on which most of the job figure depends, lost less ground against the report's baseline than any uptake estimate the report cited implies: at the designated error scale its interval for \(k\) lies below the cited-input band and excludes \(k=1\), though at the plan's upper error scale it includes it. Distributors' revenue was consistent with the forecast. These comparisons set outcomes against the report's paths. They don't estimate the decisions' effect, and they don't test the total employment forecast.

How far lower uptake reconciles the revenue outcomes with the report's chain depends on how uptake developed after June 2016, on how well the available measures match the report's BYOP uptake, and on the allowance for error in the baseline. The CRTC's entry-level share in mid-2016 ran far below the report's 2016 assumption; a 2017 survey, outside the plan's accepted sources, put starter-package adoption near the report's figure for 2017.

The partial recalculations, added after the outcome data was opened, can say only which assumed uptake paths the outcomes are compatible with. Among the paths tested, at the designated error scale, only a rise to about \lttvMorrisonUptake{} by 2019 keeps both series compatible. With uptake held at the 2016 count, the distributors' estimate lies \lttvKPredBDUObsUptakeSEs{} standard errors above its recalculated coefficient (their revenue fell further than the recalculation implies), a gap that falls below two under every calibration that sets a larger error scale; on the survey path, the recalculated coefficient for specialty and pay revenue lies \lttvKPredSpecSurveySEs{} standard errors above the estimate, since the recalculation implies far more lost ground against the baseline than appeared. Larger error scales widen the set of compatible paths. The split of distributors' revenue shows their shortfall coming through both fewer subscribers and revenue per subscriber that grew far less than the baseline assumed. If the uptake assumption was the miss, it's a quantity error of the kind \textcite[ii]{harrington1999accuracy} found behind errors in regulatory cost estimates.

Payments, closures and staff counts can be read without assuming later uptake. Affiliation payments per subscriber grew \lttvPerSubGrowth{} from 2015 to 2019, against \lttvBaseFeeGrowth{} in the report's baseline carriage fee, a growth comparison of two differently defined measures. Closures of the integrated companies' channels came out slightly below the report's \lttvReportClosuresVI{} on the plan's classification by 2015 owner (\lttvClosVIPlanClosed{} to \lttvClosVIPlanUpper{} of \lttvClosVIPlanN{} channels, against about \lttvClosVIPlanImplied{}) and around it with Corus counted as integrated, as the report's footnote 31 lists it; the independents' range is too wide to test. Staff counts, against trend, and revenue, against the report's baseline, pointed in opposite directions, a descriptive mismatch of unlike measures that can't test the employment mechanism; production and spin-off employment aren't checked.

Judged against the report rather than against outcomes, Morrison's summary to the committee departed from the report in three identifiable respects. It described an economy-wide employment estimate, more than half of it spin-off employment, as \enquote{media jobs}; it described the report's 18\%, which the report's own figures don't reproduce, as a share of current spending; and it didn't connect the job figure to the report's uptake assumption, or reconcile it with the \lttvMorrisonUptake{} estimate he cited in questioning (a figure that, like the CRTC's count and the survey, refers to the entry-level package itself rather than the report's measure, and whose base, Canadians generally or television subscribers, he didn't specify). His appearance also presented the losses as open to policy, citing the authors' proposals to reduce them, and when a member raised officials' more reassuring view he answered, \enquote{If it is right, that's good news} \citep[time marks 0915--0920]{commons2016chpc8}. What was lost between report and committee was the numerical condition on which the headline rested, a particular case of the point \textcite[1]{manski2011certitude} makes about policy analysis generally, where point predictions are common and statements of uncertainty rare.

The report deserves credit for what made this check possible. It stated its assumptions link by link, published annual paths for both scenarios, cited the range of outside estimates it chose among, gave the figures behind its headline, and said that \enquote{there is no easy way to assign probabilities to the projections underlying future scenarios} \citep[para.~111]{nordicity2015television}. It raised the possibility of low uptake itself. Publishing only the headline would have left these intermediate predictions unavailable for checking, and the useful lesson for those who commission or hear such forecasts is in that contrast. Intermediate predictions that public data can test, an uptake share or a payment flow in the second year, let a forecast be monitored before its end date, provided someone counts them: I found no official count of uptake after 2016, which is why this paper has to give more than one reading. And a statement of how wrong the baseline could be shows in advance how much a comparison like this one depends on it.

\subsection{Limits of the check}

The paper checks one forecast, and nothing here shows that stakeholder forecasts in general are biased; \textcite{simpson2014overestimate} explains why even a run of overestimates needn't show that. The verdict on specialty and pay revenue rests on how wrong the report's baseline is taken to be. I model that error as a random walk calibrated in several ways, a simple model for stating the uncertainty, and a structural break in viewing could exceed it. The intervals also treat the error scale as known, and they assume the forecast impact applies proportionally to the drifted baseline; if it applied in dollars, as in the design simulation, they'd cover \(k=1\) less often than 95\% (appendix~\ref{app:methods}). The uptake evidence is thin: two CRTC counts from the spring and summer of 2016 and one self-reported survey from 2017, which may not measure the report's BYOP uptake, so the uptake paths here illustrate the possibilities rather than bound them. The financial summaries used here can't separate closures of independent channels from continued operation under exemption, the aggregates can't identify the pass-through rate, and staff counts are a different measure from the report's modelled employment. At its historical volatility, the design analysis gave CPE too little ability to distinguish the two simulated worlds for the planned comparison.

Five findings don't depend on the model of the baseline's error: the CRTC's entry-level share in mid-2016 fell far short of the report's 2016 BYOP assumption; distributors' subscriber numbers fell faster than in either of the report's paths, if the two subscriber series stay comparable; affiliation payments to Canadian channels per subscriber grew faster than the report's baseline carriage fee; the CRTC's count of specialty and pay revenue for 2015, a year with no forecast impact, was already \lttvGapFifteenSpec{} \lttvGapFifteenSpecDir{} the report's figure, against \lttvGapFourteenSpec{} in 2014; and the testimony called an economy-wide estimate media jobs, presented the report's 18\% as a share of current spending, and omitted the uptake assumption.

\section*{Data and code}

Every number in this paper can be reproduced from the repository at \url{https://github.com/BrettRey/prediction-checker}, which holds a script that downloads the public data files the analysis reads, the frozen prediction record, the scripts that read the report's figures and check them against its text, the design analysis, and the outcome analysis, with the CRTC series used (Open Government Licence~-- Canada). Inputs encoded from documents, such as the 2015 owners taken from the CRTC's ownership charts, are recorded in the scripts with their sources, which are in the reference list. The analysis plan and each choice made after the outcome data was opened are recorded with dates in the repository. The case was first suggested in a list of candidate cases produced by ChatGPT (the latest model available on 3 October 2026, at the Pro effort level), which also proposed separating the report's technical claim from the public argument. It supplied leads and reported forecast figures, every one of which was checked against primary sources before use, and none of the calculations reported here. The analysis scripts were written with Claude Opus 5.5; independent checks of the numbers, the inference, the quotations and the fairness of the account were run with GPT-6 Astra (through Codex), and their findings were verified against the sources before any change was made.
