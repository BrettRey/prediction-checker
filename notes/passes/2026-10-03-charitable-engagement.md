**The distinctions that determine the verdict**

Three criticisms survive scrutiny:

1. **“Media jobs” misdescribes the report’s employment total.** The report expressly puts 8,300 of the 15,130 FTEs in other economic sectors.
2. **The 18% does not follow from the stated baseline and reduction.** R Table 26 explicitly supplies baseline CPE of $3,155 million; $399 million is approximately 12.6% of it.
3. **Morrison did not explain the uptake assumption behind the employment headline.** Neither his opening statement nor the subsequent answers connected 15,130 to the report’s 5%–10%–15% uptake path.

Three stronger interpretations do not survive:

1. **The entire appearance was unconditional.** Morrison discussed a policy alternative, qualified other predictions, and cited uncertain, substantially lower uptake estimates.
2. **“Direct result” necessarily misrepresents the direct/spin-off distinction.** Ordinary causal attribution and the technical category *direct employment impact* are different. Both direct and spin-off losses were attributed to policy in the report.
3. **The public forecast “failed outright.”** The paper expressly cannot test the complete employment forecast or identify the policy’s causal effect. Errors in its presentation do not supply that missing test.

**Nordicity and Peter Miller as report authors**

1. **Re-expression — partly satisfactory, with consequential omissions.**

   The paper correctly reconstructs the two principal paths, the transmission mechanisms and the employment categories. But the strongest version of the authors’ position includes their substantive purpose and their treatment of uncertainty:

   > Given continuing technological change, different implementations of unbundling could produce materially different effects on Canadian programming and employment. The selected assumptions represent the authors’ most plausible outlook, informed by available evidence; a modified policy could deliver greater choice with substantially smaller losses.

   This is supported by R §5: the scenarios are intended as the “most plausible outlooks possible,” and the authors “welcome a vigorous dialogue about these assumptions and their conclusions.” R §111 adds that “there is no easy way to assign probabilities to the projections underlying future scenarios.”

   Two qualifications particularly deserve inclusion:

   - R §§75–78 and n.40 explicitly contemplate distributors making unbundling unattractive enough that very few subscribers choose it.
   - R §§252–255 and Table 25 vary uptake, package spending, pass-through, Canadian-service shares, OTT acceleration and closures under an alternative policy.

   This third scenario is a **policy alternative with changed assumptions**, not a probability interval or a conventional sensitivity analysis under unchanged policy. Credit it accurately.

   Conversely, charity should not turn the report into an entirely hypothetical exercise. Its authors described the chosen outlook as plausible and the employment losses as “likely” (R §250). They remain accountable for those predictive judgments.

2. **Agreements — substantial, but incomplete.**

   The paper already acknowledges important things the report got right or made possible:

   - Explicit assumptions and annual paths: M475–477.
   - Integrated-company closures close to the projected proportion: M416–422.
   - Distributor revenue compatible with the forecast under the stated calibration: M281–288.
   - Channel revenue compatible with the paper’s low-uptake sensitivity calculation: M264–280.
   - Recognition of offsets within integrated companies: M411–415.

   Missing agreements are less obvious and more valuable than generic praise:

   - The report itself recognized technological pressure and reduced pricing power: R §§148–153.
   - It anticipated low uptake caused by unattractive offers: R §76 and n.40.
   - It distinguished regulated broadcasting revenue from the wider integrated company’s revenue: R §227.
   - Its closure calculation allowed both concentration among weaker services and revenue recapture by survivors: R §234.

   These show that several qualifications now used by the paper were already part of the authors’ analysis.

3. **What was learned — largely satisfactory.**

   M475–481 explicitly credits the report for making the check possible. That is substantive acknowledgment.

   It could be more exact: the paper borrows the report’s separation of technological and regulatory scenarios, its intermediate quantities, and its US comparison for evaluating the technology assumptions. R §137 explains the original Canadian/US OTT comparison. These are methodological contributions, not merely convenient disclosures.

4. **Criticism and framing — mixed.**

   The arithmetic criticism is well grounded. The conditional revenue comparisons are generally appropriately qualified.

   “Largest error” and “clearest miss,” however, overstate what a mid-2016 uptake count establishes about a forecast extending through 2020. The manuscript acknowledges both missing later counts and a measurement difference, but its summaries repeatedly omit them.

   Another problem concerns fidelity to the model. M271–273 says closures and the exemption-order component do not depend on uptake. Yet:

   - R §229 connects closures to reduced subscriber/viewer levels.
   - R §234 calculates closure losses after other revenue effects.
   - R §220 values incremental cord-cutting losses using **blended ARPU including unbundling**.

   Holding these dollar components fixed is a legitimate partial sensitivity exercise. Calling the result simply “the report’s model run at the uptake counted” overstates the reconstruction.

**Asymmetry**

The manuscript meets demanding standards in several respects: it documents changes in analysis, distinguishes descriptive comparisons from causal estimates, shows sensitivity to calibration, reports evidence favorable to the forecasters, and expressly declines to infer general stakeholder bias. Those strengths should remain.

Its remaining asymmetries are concentrated in the summaries:

- **Qualifications must survive summary on both sides.** The paper criticizes omission of assumptions while summarizing its own assumption-dependent findings as “largest error,” “failed outright,” and uptake that “fell far short.” M494–499 supplies limitations that materially narrow those formulations.
- **Compatible quantities and horizons are required symmetrically.** The paper rightly distinguishes FTEs from staff counts and present spending from a future baseline. It must be equally careful about June uptake versus an annual or eventual assumption, skinny basic versus BYOP, and “Canadians” versus subscribers.
- **Each side deserves its strongest relevant evidence.** Morrison’s policy alternative, the report’s low-uptake discussion, and Miller’s later self-correction should receive the same attention as the manuscript’s own caveats and revisions.
- **A partial sensitivity calculation needs an accurate label.** The paper should not attribute its frozen-component reconstruction to the forecasters as though it were their fully rerun model.
- **The conclusions must respect the test’s limits.** M209–210 disclaims causal identification; M463–465 disclaims a test of the employment mechanism and leaves production employment unchecked. “Failed outright” cannot bridge those gaps.
- **Revision is evidence of correction, not discredit.** The revisions recorded in Table A8 are a strength. They make reciprocal recognition of Miller’s explicit acknowledgment of error particularly appropriate. They do not immunize either party’s surviving claims from criticism.

**Objections a fair-minded reader on the report’s side could still make**

1. **“You treat possibilities we discussed as discoveries against us.”** Low uptake caused by unattractive offers appears in R §76 and n.40. The paper should distinguish the selected numerical assumption’s performance from the breadth of the authors’ foresight.

2. **“You have not established that the conditions persisted unchanged.”** The testimony explicitly advocated mitigation; the report identified policy-dependent outcomes. The manuscript cannot rule out policy or market adaptation merely by showing that an initial path differed from outcomes. This does not require a new investigation to complete this audit: narrow the conclusion to what was checked.

3. **“You treat an early count as the decisive error behind a later forecast.”** The early count is informative, but missing later uptake prevents a full-horizon verdict and limits the flat-uptake reconstruction.

4. **“You use sector aggregates to answer a more uneven concern.”** Integrated-company totals and broad channel revenues can coexist with serious harm to independent services or local news. The paper acknowledges some aggregation limits, but “public argument” remains broad enough to imply that these concerns were answered.

5. **“Our 18% error is being counted again as Morrison’s exaggeration.”** Separate the inherited arithmetic problem from his additional change to “what now exists.”

6. **“‘Direct result’ is being read in a technical sense we did not use.”** This objection is persuasive. Remove the category-based criticism of those words.

7. **“You imply we never reconsidered anything.”** The absence of a systematic evaluation of this forecast can be reported alongside L n.88 and n.196. Their existence warrants acknowledgment without claiming they settle the forecast.

8. **“You have not tested the headline you pronounce a failure.”** This is the strongest objection. A revenue-path discrepancy, an employment-label error and an unexplained percentage support specific corrections. They do not establish that 15,130 economy-wide FTE losses attributable to policy did not occur.

A defensible replacement for the central public-argument verdict would be:

> Morrison’s opening summary described an economy-wide employment estimate as “media jobs,” repeated an 18% figure that does not follow from the report’s stated CPE totals, and did not explain the uptake assumption underlying the employment projection. The wider appearance included qualifications and policy alternatives, but did not reconcile the headline with the lower uptake estimate he cited in questioning. These are limitations of the presentation; the present analysis does not directly test the total employment effect.
