                                                         Abstract
               In April 2016 a House of Commons committee heard that new television rules from the
           Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission (CRTC) would cost 15,130
           media jobs by 2020. The figure came from a commissioned study detailed enough to check. I
           fixed the forecast record and analysis plan before collecting outcomes and compared two of the
           study’s three scenarios with CRTC data for 2016–2019. As planned, specialty and pay chan-
           nels’ revenue, on which most of the job figure depends, was inconsistent with the forecast path
           at the pre-stated error scale, and distributors’ revenue was consistent with it. The study’s key
           input, uptake of an entry-level package, was 1.6% in mid-2016 against an assumed 5%; a 2017
           survey, outside the plan’s accepted sources, put it near the study’s 10% for that year. In analyses
           added after seeing outcomes, the study’s chain recalculated at the CRTC’s count fits channel rev-
           enue but not distributors’ losses; at the survey’s level it comes closer for distributors but predicts
           channel losses that didn’t occur. The testimony called an economy-wide estimate media jobs, de-
           scribed the study’s unreproducible 18% as a share of current spending, and didn’t connect the
           job figure to the uptake assumption.

     The number came from an economic-impact study by the consultancy Nordicity and Peter Miller,
commissioned by Friends of Canadian Broadcasting and four other organizations in the sector; the
report attributes its conclusions to the authors, not the commissioning groups (Nordicity & Miller,
2015). It modelled what the rules would do to distributors’ and channels’ revenue, to spending on
Canadian programming, and, through input-output multipliers, to jobs and GDP through 2020,
stating its assumptions link by link. CRTC series on revenue, programming spending and staff are
now available for those years. My recorded searches of scholarly databases and the web found no pub-
lished comparison of the forecast’s numerical paths with outcomes (the queries are in the replication
files). Miller’s later work for the CRTC discusses what the decisions led to, and acknowledges an
error in a separate prediction of station closures (Miller, 2022, n. 88, n. 196).
     Economists have long compared what was predicted before a regulation with what happened after
it. Harrington et al. (1999, p. ii) found that ex ante estimates of direct costs exceeded actual costs for
12 of 25 environmental and occupational-safety rules and fell short for 6, with the errors in quantities
“driven by both baseline and compliance issues”. A later set of case studies suggested “a slight tendency
to overestimate both costs and benefits” (Morgenstern, 2018, p. 285). Simpson (2014) warns that a
preponderance of overestimates need not show bias, since skewed cost distributions could produce
one anyway; his review notes that the few studies of industry’s own estimates found them above
outcomes in most cases (Simpson, 2014, pp. 319–320).
     The work I’ve cited concerns mainly regulators’ estimates of compliance costs and benefits. This
case is a different kind of forecast: an economy-wide estimate of jobs and output, made for stake-
holder organizations and presented to legislators in testimony. In policy analysis generally, “Point
predictions are common and expressions of uncertainty are rare” (Manski, 2011, p. 1).
     A close precedent is Kane (2026), who checked the admissions and enrolment forecasts made by
universities and by Harvard’s expert during the litigation that ended in the US Supreme Court’s de-
cision in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard. He reports results against more than one baseline,
and he separates the expert’s technical estimate from the forecasts the institutions put to the Court.
I make the same separation. The report’s technical claim is a conditional forecast, which its authors
offered as “the most plausible outlooks possible given available information” (Nordicity & Miller,
2015, para. iii): if the rules took effect and its assumptions held, revenue, programming spending
and jobs would follow given paths. The public argument is what the committee was told.
     I fixed the forecast record before downloading any CRTC outcome file, and ran a simulation,
before opening those files, of whether they could tell a world with the forecast effect from one without
it (Gelman & Carlin, 2014; Gelman et al., 2020). The main outcome measure is a single number, k:
the observed shortfall below the report’s own no-reform baseline over 2016–2019, the years before
the pandemic, as a multiple of its forecast impact, so that k = 0 is the report’s no-reform path and
k = 1 its forecast.
     The report’s main input was uptake. Subscribers taking the entry-level package with a few chosen
channels were to be 5% of all subscribers in 2016, 10% in 2017 and 15% from 2018, figures chosen
from outside estimates of 10% to 35%. The CRTC counted 1.6% at the end of June 2016, before every
channel had to be offered singly as well as in small packages, and published no later count. A survey by
the Media Technology Monitor, released in August 2017, put adoption of starter packages at “just

    described an economy-wide estimate, more than half of it spin-off employment in other sectors, as
    “media jobs”. It described the report’s 18%, which the report’s own figures don’t reproduce, as a share
    of “what now exists” rather than of projected 2020 spending. And it didn’t connect the job figure
    to the uptake assumption, or reconcile it with the lower uptake estimate he cited when asked. His
    appearance also presented the losses as open to policy, citing the authors’ proposals to reduce them.

2   The decision, the forecast and the testimony
    In March 2015 the CRTC told television distributors to offer, by March 2016, an entry-level service
    “priced at no more than $25 (not including equipment) per month” (CRTC, 2015, para. 26). Every
    discretionary channel was to be available on its own or in small packages by March 2016, and in
2   The decision, the forecast and the testimony                                                          4


both forms by December 2016 (CRTC, 2015, para. 47). The policy came out of the Let’s Talk TV
proceeding, at which, by one later account, “virtually all parties expressed concerns about potential
impacts of unbundling” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. xxii, p. 5).
    In a report dated December 2015, and released by its authors’ later account in January 2016
(Miller, 2022), Nordicity and Peter Miller published Canadian Television 2020. It was prepared for
ACTRA, the Canadian Media Guild, the Directors Guild of Canada, Friends of Canadian Broadcast-
ing and Unifor (Nordicity & Miller, 2015). It modelled two worlds from 2015 to 2020: a baseline
with “technological and consumer behaviour changes, assuming that the pre-existing broadcasting
regulatory regime remains”, and the same world with the CRTC’s decisions overlaid (Nordicity &
Miller, 2015, para. ii, p. 1). Every headline number is the gap between the two. A third scenario, with
the decisions modified, isn’t checked here.
    By 2020, the report concluded, the decisions were “likely to result in a loss of 15,130 FTEs of
employment in the Canadian economy”, counted in full-time equivalents, along with $1,411.1 mil-
lion of GDP. Of those jobs, 6,830 were directly in broadcasting and production and 8,300 in other
sectors (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. xliv and Table 1, p. 12). Those figures sit at the end of a chain.
Subscribers move to cheaper unbundled packages; distributors lose retail revenue and pass most of
the loss to Canadian channels as lower wholesale fees; weaker channels close; spending on Canadian
programming falls; and input-output multipliers turn the spending into jobs and GDP.
    The report states the assumptions that drive each link. Subscribers taking the entry-level service
with a few chosen channels make up 5% of all subscribers in 2016, 10% in 2017 and 15% from 2018
(Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 18, p. 76). Distributors pass “75% of their retail revenue loss to
Canadian programming services”, having attributed 86% of that loss to Canadian services (Nordicity
& Miller, 2015, paras. 206–207, p. 77). By 2020, 10% of the large integrated companies’ Category
A and B channels close, and 25% of the independents’ (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. 232–233,
p. 85).
    By 2020 the model takes $970 million (23%) a year out of specialty and pay channels’ revenue
and $858 million (9%) out of distributors’ revenue, against the baseline (Nordicity & Miller, 2015,
paras. xxvi–xxvii).
2.1 What the committee heard
On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting, accompanied by Miller, put the
report to the Commons Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage. Its key findings, he said, were
that “by 2020, some 15,130 media jobs will be lost”, along with “a $400-million drop in Canadian
program expenditures” that was “18% of what now exists”, “all of this as the direct result of Let’s Talk
TV regulatory changes”. He added that the authors had “advanced proposals to reduce the negative
impact of the CRTC’s decisions by as much as 75%” (House of Commons Standing Committee on
Canadian Heritage, 2016, time marks 0900–0905).
    Two of these phrases depart from the report. The 15,130 FTEs are economy-wide, and 8,300 of
them are spin-off employment in other sectors, so “media jobs” misdescribes them; and the report
describes its 18% as a share of projected 2020 baseline spending, not of current spending. The $400
million rounds the report’s $399 million, and “direct result” attributes the losses to the decisions, as
the report does, though “will be lost” is firmer than the report’s “likely”. The 18% itself is the report’s,
and its own figures don’t reproduce it. The report’s own baseline-scenario figures put 2020 Canadian
programming expenditure at $3,155 million, so its $399 million reduction is 12.6% of baseline, not

    the 18% its text states (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 239, Figs. 8, 9, 43). Against the report’s own
    forecast for 2015, the reduction is 12.2%, and against what existed when Morrison spoke, the CRTC’s

3   How the forecast was checked
    The prediction record (forecasters, quantities, paths by year, stated assumptions) was committed be-
    fore any CRTC outcome series was downloaded. I then re-read the report’s two paths from the data
    labels on its charts, checking every value against the totals in its text, and ran a design analysis by fake-
    data simulation before opening any CRTC outcome file (Gelman & Carlin, 2014; Gelman et al.,
    2020). It asked, for each forecast quantity, whether the outcome could separate a world in which the
    report’s impact occurred from one in which it didn’t, given that the baseline itself could be wrong.
         That last clause is the hard part. In retrospective analysis of regulation, “developing a credible
    counterfactual is the most demanding aspect”, and the baselines assumed beforehand “must be re-
    visited” to see whether outside factors changed the result (Morgenstern, 2018, p. 286). Here the
    counterfactual is the report’s own no-reform baseline, itself a forecast of how television would have
    fared under streaming without the reforms. So the check treats the baseline’s error as unknown, puts
    a range on it, and reports results across that range, much as Kane (2026) reports against more than
    one baseline.
         It depended on the quantity. With the baseline’s error set at the volatility of the report’s own
    2010–2014 data, specialty and pay revenue would be classified correctly in about 99% of simulated
    worlds and distributors’ revenue in about 86%. Canadian programming expenditure, whose histor-
    ical volatility was 9.1% a year, came out at 63%, so it was set aside in advance as descriptive.
         The outcome measure is a single number, k: the observed shortfall below the report’s baseline
    over 2016–2019, the four years before the pandemic, as a multiple of the report’s own forecast impact,
    so that k = 0 is the report’s no-reform path and k = 1 its forecast. In logs, log Bt −log Yt = k δt +et ,
    where Bt is the report’s baseline, Yt the outcome, δt the report’s forecast shortfall, and et the baseline’s
    error (positive when the baseline overstates what would have happened without the decisions), mod-
    elled as a random walk from 2014. Because that error accumulates from year to year, the estimate
    rests on changes in the gap between outcome and baseline rather than on its level: specialty and pay
    revenue’s k is positive although that revenue stayed above the baseline in every year, because its excess
    over the baseline shrank, the direction the forecast predicted. The report’s paths and the CRTC series
    are both in nominal dollars, and since k compares them year by year, deflating both by the same price
    index leaves it unchanged.
         The 95% interval for k comes from generalized least squares under that model, and it reflects only
    the assumed error in the baseline, so its width depends on how large that error is taken to be. The
    scale of that error is calibrated from an outside comparison, described below, rather than estimated
    from four years of outcomes, and the CRTC’s administrative totals are treated as measured without
    error (appendix A; inputs in table A2). The interval treats that scale as known and doesn’t carry the
    4   What happened                                                                                          6


    uncertainty in the scale itself. As an illustration, if the historical volatility were treated as an estimate
    from four independent, normal annual changes, an interval using the t distribution with three degrees
    of freedom would be about 62% wider, and the distributors’ interval would then include zero; even
    that illustration doesn’t carry the uncertainty of the actual calibration rule.
         k compares the outcome with the report’s two forecast paths; it isn’t an estimate of the decisions’
    causal effect, which would need a better counterfactual than the report’s own baseline. A band of
    k built from the estimates the report cites (uptake between 10% and 35%, closures up to 2.5 times
    the assumed rate) counts as consistent with the forecast; the band is my construction from those
    estimates, not one the report published. An outcome below the forecast path was to be reported as
    the forecast exceeded, never as confirmed.
         The outcome series alone can’t identify how wrong the baseline was, so the plan fixed an external
    check: the forecast of US Netflix subscribers that the report used for its US comparison (from Trefis),
    assumed unaffected by Canadian regulation, against Netflix’s reported US paid memberships (Net-
    flix, Inc., 2019). The report forecast 57.0 million for 2018 and Netflix reported 58.5 million, which
    by the pre-stated rule puts the error at 1.3% a year, below the historical volatility of both revenue
    series. The CRTC series reproduce the report’s 2012–2014 values within 2% (CRTC, 2017, 2021).

observing prices or identifying causes. Subscriber losses exceed those in both of the report’s paths
compared here, and stagnant revenue per subscriber could reflect pricing, cord shaving to smaller
packages, or the reforms themselves.

    counts run between 1.3% and 1.5% below the report’s figures in every year from 2012 to 2015, a
    standing difference between the two series, with no new divergence in 2015. The 2016 and 2020
    editions of the CRTC’s summaries differ on 2016 by 0.1% for channels and 0.5% for distributors,
    so revisions between editions can’t account for the gap. Allowing a level offset in the model, which
    absorbs both, gives k̂ = 0.20 (standard error 0.18) for specialty and pay revenue and 1.23 (0.44) for
    distributors’ revenue, leaving both pre-stated readings as they were; the distributors’ counted-uptake
    comparison falls to 1.8 standard errors.

5   The links in the chain
    The report’s numerical inputs can be compared with observed quantities without estimating a causal
    effect. The input on which most of the forecast depends is uptake. The report took its 15% from out-
    side estimates, the highest being Oliver Wyman’s finding that “as many as 35% of subscribers would
    be willing to switch to a skinny-basic/BYOP option” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 201, p. 75).
    The CRTC counted more than 66,000 subscribers to the entry-level package five weeks after launch
    (CRTC, 2016a) and 177,000 by 30 June 2016 (CRTC, 2016b, para. 12), about 0.6% and 1.6% of
    the 2016 subscriber total, against the report’s 5% for 2016. The CRTC published no later count. A
    survey by the Media Technology Monitor, released in August 2017, put adoption of starter packages
5   The links in the chain                                                                              11


at “just over 1 in 10 TV subscribers” and reported that 29% of subscribers were adding individual
channels or bundles to their packages (Media Technology Monitor, 2017). Surveys aren’t among
the sources the analysis plan accepted for uptake, and this one is self-reported and may not match
the report’s measure, the entry-level package with chosen channels; it’s reported beside the planned
reading, with the results under both. Low uptake in 2016 may partly reflect how the package was
sold, a possibility the report itself raised, that distributors might offer unbundling in a way “relatively
unattractive from a price-value equation, so that very few Canadians opt for it” (Nordicity & Miller,
2015, para. 78, n. 40, p. 34). The CRTC’s hearing of September 2016 examined “the practices certain
providers have adopted in offering” it (CRTC, 2016b, para. 5). Either way, uptake in mid-2016 was
far below the level on which most of the forecast rested; whether it stayed there is the question the
survey reopens.
     The report also assumed a path for what distributors pay Canadian channels. In its no-reform
baseline the monthly fee per subscriber for Canadian specialty and pay services rises 1.6% from 2015
to 2019 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 14, p. 67), and under the decisions it falls below that path
as distributors pass on 75% of the retail loss attributed to Canadian services. What distributors paid
Canadian channels rose from $3,014 million in 2015 to $3,125 million in 2019 while subscribers fell,
so per subscriber it rose 10.5%, and the Canadian share of all affiliation payments stayed near 87.8%
(CRTC, 2019, Table U-T11). After inflation the total fell 3.5% and the payment per subscriber rose
2.9%; the comparison with the report’s fee path needs no such adjustment, since that path is nominal
too. The two fee measures differ by definition, so their growth is compared, not their levels. At uptake
near the CRTC’s count there would have been little loss to pass on, and the comparison would mainly
show that payments grew faster than the report’s baseline assumed; at the survey’s level, the chain
implies fee cuts that didn’t appear. Either way, payments ended above the report’s no-reform path,
where the decisions scenario puts them below it.
     The aggregates don’t show why fees rose. Some of the payments stay inside the integrated groups,
and the report itself expected the exemption order’s impact on those groups to be partly offset by
their streaming and broadband revenue (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 227, p. 83). The CRTC’s
wholesale code, scheduled to take effect in January 2016 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, p. 73, n. 82), is
in both of the report’s scenarios (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, p. 72, n. 80), so its fee path already allows
for it.
     For the integrated companies, closures came out close to the report: between 8% and 11% of
their Category A and B channels stopped operating by 2019–2020, against the report’s 10%. For the
independents the bounds, 6% to 33%, contain the report’s 25% but are too wide to test it, because
channels that left licensing for exemption can’t be told apart from channels that closed. Both ranges
classify Corus, which owns the former Shaw Media channels in the 2016 data, as independent; coun-
ted as integrated, as the analysis plan also required, it gives 6% to 11% for the integrated companies
and 7% to 44% for the independents. Canadian programming expenditure, read only descriptively,
stayed at or above the baseline path through 2019.
     For two sectors, the CRTC’s staff counts allow a descriptive comparison with the last link, from
revenue to jobs (CRTC, 2017, 2021). The report gives its direct FTE losses by sector against a baseline
whose employment path it doesn’t publish, so the comparison is with the change from 2015 and with
the 2012–2015 trend carried forward, a crude stand-in for that baseline. Channels’ staff fell from
5,899 in 2015 to 4,403 in 2019, 1,282 below the trend, against a forecast direct loss of 800 by 2019.
    6   What the case shows                                                                               12


    Distributors’ staff stayed near its pre-2015 trend, 396 below it in 2018 and 1,834 above it in 2019,
    against a forecast direct loss of 2,910 by 2019 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 22, p. 91). The 2019
    figure is a one-year jump that reverses the next year, which may be a reporting discontinuity.
         Staff counts and revenue don’t move together as the chain implies in either sector: channels cut
    staff while their revenue stayed above the baseline, and distributors’ staffing stayed near trend while
    their revenue fell (annual series in table A6). It’s a descriptive mismatch, not a test of the model’s
    employment prediction. The counts aren’t the report’s modelled full-time equivalents, the distrib-
    utor series changes from an FTE count to a staff count between editions (the two differ by 302 in
    2016), and a straight-line trend is a weak counterfactual. The direction of the mismatch holds with
    the distributors’ 2018 figure in place of 2019.

6   What the case shows
    Read as a conditional forecast, the report did better than its headline, and its three questions get
    different answers. Whether its main input failed depends on which uptake evidence is right: the
    CRTC’s count in mid-2016 ran far below the report’s assumption, while a 2017 survey, outside the
    plan’s accepted sources, put uptake near the report’s figure for that year. Whether its mechanism
    worked depends on the same question. If uptake stayed near the 2016 count, channel revenue is
    consistent with the report’s chain recalculated at that uptake, and distributors lost more than the
    chain explains, through both fewer subscribers and revenue per subscriber that grew far less than the
    baseline assumed. If uptake reached the survey’s level, distributors’ revenue comes closer to the chain,
    which then produces more than half of their shortfall, and channel revenue held up far better than the
    chain allows, while the payments distributors made to Canadian channels per subscriber rose above
    the report’s no-reform path instead of falling below it.
         Under either reading one link departs from the chain, and which one depends on an uptake figure
    the CRTC never published after 2016. If the 2016 count was the miss, it’s the kind of quantity error
    Harrington et al. (1999, p. ii) found behind misses in regulatory cost estimates.
         The jobs figure put to Parliament rests on that chain, and the report divides it two ways (table A7;
    Nordicity and Miller, 2015, paras. 246, 248, 250, Tables 22–23, pp. 90–92). By kind of effect, 6,830
    of the 15,130 FTEs are direct jobs in broadcasting and production and 8,300 are spin-off jobs in other
    sectors. By the sector whose losses give rise to them, 7,180 are attributed to independent production
    (2,830 direct, 4,350 spin-off) and 7,950 to broadcasting (4,000 direct, 3,950 spin-off). The 10,060
    (66%) attributed to specialty and pay services (2,880) and to production depend in the model on
    Canadian channels’ revenue and programming spending.
         The staff counts don’t move with that chain: channels shed more staff than the forecast’s direct
    FTE loss although their revenue stayed above the baseline, and distributors’ staffing stayed near trend
    although their revenue fell. These are descriptive comparisons of unlike measures, which can’t test
    the report’s employment mechanism. This paper doesn’t estimate the spin-off employment, and
    production employment isn’t checked here.
         Morrison’s summary to the committee departed from the report in three identifiable respects. It
    described an economy-wide employment estimate, more than half of it spin-off employment, as “me-
    dia jobs”; it described the report’s 18%, which the report’s own figures don’t reproduce, as a share
    of current spending; and it didn’t connect the job figure to the report’s uptake assumption, or re-
    concile it with the 4% estimate he cited when asked, a level at which the recalculated chain gives a
6   What the case shows                                                                                13


projected k of 0.40 (his figure, like the CRTC’s count and the survey, refers to the entry-level pack-
age itself, not quite the report’s measure). His appearance also presented the losses as open to policy,
citing the authors’ proposals to reduce them, and when a member raised officials’ more reassuring
view he answered, “If it is right, that’s good news” (House of Commons Standing Committee on Ca-
nadian Heritage, 2016, time marks 0915–0920). What was lost between report and committee was
the numerical condition on which the headline rested, a particular case of the point Manski (2011,
p. 1) makes about policy analysis generally, where point predictions are common and statements of
uncertainty rare. These are limits of the presentation; the paper doesn’t test the total employment
effect.
     The report deserves credit for what made this check possible. It stated its assumptions link by
link, published annual paths for both scenarios, cited the range of outside estimates it chose among,
gave the figures behind its headline, and said plainly that “there is no easy way to assign probabilit-
ies to the projections underlying future scenarios” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 111). It raised
the possibility of low uptake itself. Its commissioning organizations made that disclosure possible.
Publishing only the headline would have left these intermediate predictions unavailable for checking,
and the useful lesson for those who commission or hear such forecasts is in that contrast. Intermedi-
ate predictions that public data can test, an uptake share or a payment flow in the second year, let a
forecast be monitored before its end date, provided someone counts them: a single official count of
uptake is why this paper has to give two readings. And a statement of how wrong the baseline could
be shows in advance how much the headline depends on it.
6.1 Limits of the check
The paper checks one forecast, and nothing here shows that stakeholder forecasts in general are biased;
Simpson (2014) explains why even a run of overestimates needn’t show that. The verdict on channel
revenue rests on how wrong the report’s technology-only baseline is taken to be. I model that error as
a random walk calibrated in several ways, a simple model for stating the uncertainty, and a structural
break in viewing could exceed it. The intervals also treat the scale of that error as known, and they
assume the forecast impact applies proportionally to the drifted baseline; if it applied in dollars, as in
the design simulation, they’d cover the forecast in about 87% of cases for channels and 93% for distrib-
utors rather than 95%, and the channel estimate would lie about 3.8 simulated standard deviations
from the forecast rather than 5.0 standard errors. The uptake evidence is thin: one CRTC count from
mid-2016 and one self-reported survey from 2017, which may not measure what the report meant by
uptake, so the two readings bracket the possibilities without settling them. The financial summaries
used here can’t separate closures of independent channels from moves to exemption, the aggregates
can’t identify the pass-through rate, and the staff counts are a different measure from the report’s
modelled employment. At its historical volatility, the design analysis gave Canadian programming
expenditure too little power for the planned test.
    Five findings don’t depend on the model of the baseline’s error: uptake in mid-2016 fell far short
of the report’s 2016 assumption; distributors’ subscriber numbers fell faster than in either of the
report’s paths, if the two subscriber series stay comparable; payments to Canadian channels per sub-
scriber ended above the report’s own no-reform path; the CRTC’s count of specialty and pay revenue
for 2015, a year with no forecast impact, was already 2.5% above the report’s figure, against 0.8% in
2014; and the testimony described the report’s figures in the three ways above that the report doesn’t
support.

   Before any CRTC outcome file was opened, I simulated each quantity 20,000 times (seed 20261003)
