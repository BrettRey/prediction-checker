Checking a forecast put to Parliament: unbundling
Canadian television, 2016–2019
Brett Reynolds *
Humber Polytechnic & University of Toronto
4th October 2026

Abstract
I check a forecast put to a Canadian parliamentary committee: a commissioned study’s estimate that new television rules would cost 15,130 jobs by 2020. Under a plan fixed before
collecting outcomes, I compare its intermediate predictions with regulator data for 2016–2019;
neither the job total nor the rules’ causal effect is tested. At the error scale the plan set for the
study’s baseline, the channel revenue on which most of the job figure depends lost less ground
against that baseline than any estimate of package uptake the study cited implies, and distributors’ revenue was consistent with the forecast. Under the largest error scales some calibrations
give, both verdicts become inconclusive. How far the uptake assumption explains the channel
result depends on uptake after mid-2016, when the regulator counted 1.6% of subscribers on
the new entry-level package, against the study’s 5% taking it with chosen channels; a 2017 survey outside the plan’s sources put adoption near the study’s figure for that year. The testimony
called an economy-wide estimate media jobs and omitted the uptake assumption.

Introduction
On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting told a committee of the House
of Commons that the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission’s (CRTC’s)
new rules for television would, “by 2020”, cost “some 15,130 media jobs”, “all of this as the direct
result of Let’s Talk TV regulatory changes” (House of Commons Standing Committee on Canadian
Heritage, 2016, time marks 0900–0905). The rules, the outcome of the CRTC’s Let’s Talk TV proceeding, required cable, satellite and internet-protocol (IPTV) distributors to offer a $25 entry-level

package and to sell discretionary channels singly or in small bundles (Canadian Radio-television and
Telecommunications Commission [CRTC], 2015).
The number came from an economic-impact study by the consultancy Nordicity and Peter Miller
(2015), commissioned by Friends of Canadian Broadcasting and four other organizations in the sector; the report attributes its analysis and conclusions to the authors, not necessarily the commissioning groups. It modelled what the rules would do to distributors’ and channels’ revenue, to spending
on Canadian programming, and, through input-output multipliers, to jobs and GDP through 2020,
stating its assumptions link by link. CRTC series on revenue, programming spending and staff are
now available for those years. My recorded searches of scholarly databases and the web found no published comparison of the forecast’s numerical paths with outcomes (the queries are in the replication
files). Miller’s own later work for the CRTC (2022, n. 88, n. 196) discusses what the decisions led to,
and acknowledges an error in a separate prediction of station closures.
This paper compares the report’s revenue predictions for specialty and pay services and for distributors with outcomes through 2019, adds descriptive checks of uptake, payments, closures and
staff counts, and compares the report with what the committee was told. It tests neither the total
employment forecast nor the decisions’ causal effect.
The report’s main input is uptake, the share of subscribers taking an entry-level package with
chosen channels. The main findings are these:
– In the comparisons the plan fixed in advance, allowing for error in the report’s own baseline at the
scale the plan designated for each series, specialty and pay revenue, on which most of the job figure
depends, stayed above the report’s baseline and lost less ground against it than any of the outside
uptake estimates the report cited (10% to 35%) implies. Distributors’ revenue was consistent with
the forecast. Under the largest error scales that alternative calibrations give, both verdicts become
inconclusive.
– The report assumed 5% uptake for 2016, far above the 1.6% of subscribers the CRTC counted on
the entry-level package in mid-2016. A 2017 survey, outside the sources the analysis plan accepted,
suggests starter-package adoption later came close to the report’s 10% for 2017.
– In partial recalculations of the report’s chain from uptake to revenue, added after the outcome data
was opened, only a low uptake path among those tested is compatible with both revenue estimates
at the designated scales.
– The testimony called an economy-wide employment estimate media jobs, presented the report’s
share of projected 2020 spending on Canadian programming as a share of current spending, and
omitted the uptake assumption. The report’s own totals put that share at 12.6%, not its stated
18%.
Economists have long compared what was predicted before a regulation with what happened after
it. Harrington et al. (2000, p. 297) found that ex ante estimates of total direct costs exceeded actual
costs for 14 of 28 environmental and occupational-safety rules and fell short for 3, with the quantity
errors “driven both by difficulties in determining the baseline and by incomplete compliance”, and
a later set of case studies suggested “a slight tendency to overestimate both costs and benefits” (Morgenstern, 2018, p. 285). Simpson (2014, p. 322) warns that a preponderance of overestimates need
not show bias, since skewed cost distributions could produce one anyway; his review notes that the
few studies of industry’s own estimates found them above outcomes in most cases (pp. 319–321).
That work concerns mainly regulators’ estimates of compliance costs and benefits. This case is

a different kind of forecast: an economy-wide estimate of jobs and output, made for stakeholder
organizations and presented to legislators in testimony. Such estimates have a critical literature of
their own. Crompton (2006, p. 67) documents how the job and output totals of commissioned
economic-impact studies are inflated through multipliers and generous assumptions about which
spending is new, and independent studies of sports facilities have found no significant effect on local
development (Siegfried & Zimbalist, 2000, p. 103). This paper doesn’t test the report’s multipliers;
it checks the predictions they were applied to. In policy analysis generally, “Point predictions are
common and expressions of uncertainty are rare” (Manski, 2011, p. 1).
A close precedent is Kane (2026), who checked the admissions and enrolment forecasts made
by universities and by Harvard’s expert during the litigation that ended in the US Supreme Court’s
decision in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard. He separates the expert’s technical estimate
from the forecasts the institutions put to the Court, and I make the same separation. The report’s
technical claim is a conditional forecast, which its authors offered as “the most plausible outlooks
possible given available information” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. iii): if the rules took effect and
its assumptions held, revenue, programming spending and jobs would follow given paths. The public
argument is what the committee was told.
For the technical claim, I fixed the forecast record before downloading any CRTC outcome file,
and ran a simulation, before opening those files, of whether they could tell a world with the forecast effect from one without it (Gelman & Carlin, 2014; Gelman et al., 2020). The main outcome
measure is a forecast scale k for each revenue series, estimated over 2016–2019, the years before the
pandemic: k = 0 corresponds to the report’s baseline and k = 1 to its forecast, under a model that
allows the baseline itself to be wrong. Analyses added after the outcome data was opened are labelled
as such and listed in order in table B8.
Section 2 sets out the decision, the forecast and its presentation to Parliament. Section 3 describes
how outcomes are compared with the report’s paths, section 4 the revenue outcomes and their sensitivity, and section 5 payments, closures and staff. Section 6 says what the evidence establishes and
what it doesn’t. All amounts are in nominal Canadian dollars, as the report’s are; changes stated after
inflation use the consumer price index (Statistics Canada, 2026).

The forecast and its presentation to Parliament
In March 2015 the CRTC told television distributors to offer, by March 2016, an entry-level service
“priced at no more than $25 (not including equipment) per month” (CRTC, 2015, para. 26). Every
discretionary channel was to be available on its own or in small packages by March 2016, and in
both forms by December 2016 (CRTC, 2015, para. 47). The policy came out of the Let’s Talk TV
proceeding, at which, by one later account, “virtually all parties expressed concerns about potential
impacts of unbundling” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. xxii, p. 5).
Nordicity and Peter Miller’s Canadian Television 2020, dated December 2015, was prepared for
ACTRA (the Alliance of Canadian Cinema, Television and Radio Artists), the Canadian Media
Guild, the Directors Guild of Canada, Friends of Canadian Broadcasting and Unifor. It modelled
two worlds from 2015 to 2020: a baseline with “technological and consumer behaviour changes,
assuming that the pre-existing broadcasting regulatory regime remains”, and the same world with the
CRTC’s decisions overlaid (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. ii, p. 1). I call these the report’s baseline
and its reform scenario; the forecast is the gap between them, and a quantity’s path under the reform

By 2020, the report concluded, the decisions were “likely to result in a loss of 15,130 FTEs of employment in the Canadian economy”, counted in full-time equivalents (FTEs), along with $1,411.1
million of GDP. Of those jobs, 6,830 were directly in broadcasting and production and 8,300 were
spin-off employment, the indirect and induced employment in other sectors (Nordicity & Miller,
2015, para. xliv and Table 1 with its note, p. 12). The report also divides the total by the sector whose
losses give rise to it: 7,180 to independent production (2,830 direct, 4,350 spin-off) and 7,950 to
broadcasting (4,000 direct, 3,950 spin-off), as table B7 sets out (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. 246,
248, 250, Tables 22–23, pp. 90–92). The 10,060 (66%) attributed to production and, within broadcasting, to specialty and pay services depend in the model on those services’ revenue and on spending
on Canadian programming, which is why specialty and pay revenue is the main test below.
The job and GDP figures sit at the end of a chain. Subscribers move to cheaper unbundled packages; distributors lose retail revenue and pass most of the loss to Canadian channels as lower wholesale
fees; weaker channels close; spending on Canadian programming falls; and input-output multipliers
turn the spending into jobs and GDP. These are gross figures. The report links them to reductions
in broadcasting and production revenue (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 245), and I found no offset
for the spending that subscribers who pay less redirect elsewhere. The report states the assumptions
that drive each link. Subscribers taking the entry-level package with chosen channels, which the report calls Build Your Own Package (BYOP), make up 5% of all subscribers in 2016, 10% in 2017 and
15% from 2018 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 202, Table 18, p. 76); I call this the report’s BYOP
uptake. Distributors attribute 86% of the retail revenue they lose to Canadian services and pass 75%
of that part on to them as lower wholesale fees (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. 206–207, p. 77).
By 2020, 10% of the Category A and B specialty channels owned by the large vertically integrated
companies, which own both distributors and channels, close, and 25% of the independents’ (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. 232–233, p. 85); the report’s footnote 31, citing the CRTC, names the
English-language vertically integrated entities as Bell, Rogers and Shaw/Corus.
By 2020 the reform path runs $970 million (23%) a year below the baseline for the revenue of specialty and pay services (“channels”, for short, below) and $858 million (9%) below it for distributors’
revenue (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. xxvi–xxvii). Canadian programming expenditure (CPE),
which in the report includes distributors’ contributions as well as programming services’ spending,
runs $399 million below.
The report divides the revenue shortfall into four components: unbundling; the change from
requiring a preponderance of Canadian channels among those each subscriber receives to among
those offered, with the end of access rules, which it models as Canadian channels’ share of BYOP
subscribers’ wholesale fees falling from 86% to 50% by 2020 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 212);
an exemption order under which streaming services such as Crave TV and Shomi, until then offered
only to TV subscribers, could compete for all viewers, which it expected to “materially increase cord
cutting and cord shaving” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. xxiii), that is, cancellations and downgrades of TV subscriptions; and channel closures (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Figs. 2–3).
Unbundling and the preponderance change act through BYOP subscribers and so scale with uptake; the report sets the exemption order and closures separately, though it values them partly from
the other two (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. 220, 234).

2.1 What the committee heard
On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting, accompanied by Miller, put the
report to the Commons Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage. Its key findings, he said, were
that “by 2020, some 15,130 media jobs will be lost”, along with “a $400-million drop in Canadian
program expenditures” that was “18% of what now exists”, “all of this as the direct result of Let’s Talk
TV regulatory changes”. He added that the authors had “advanced proposals to reduce the negative
impact of the CRTC’s decisions by as much as 75%” (House of Commons Standing Committee on
Canadian Heritage, 2016, time marks 0900–0905).
Two of these phrases depart from the report. The 15,130 FTEs are economy-wide, and 8,300 of
them are spin-off employment in other sectors, so “media jobs” misdescribes them; and the report
describes its 18% as a share of projected 2020 baseline spending, not of current spending. The $400
million rounds the report’s $399 million, and “direct result” attributes the losses to the decisions, as
the report does, though “will be lost” is firmer than the report’s “likely”. The 18% itself is the report’s,
though its figures don’t reproduce it: its baseline puts 2020 CPE at $3,155 million, so its $399 million
reduction is 12.6% of baseline, not the 18% its text states (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 239, Figs. 8,
9, 43). Against the report’s own forecast for 2015, the reduction is 12.2%, and against what existed
when Morrison spoke, the CRTC’s count of 2015 spending ($3,286 million), it’s 12.1% (CRTC,
2017). None of the 12 shares tested, built from the report’s figures over 9 denominators and its two
impact figures, gives 18% (table B1).
Responding to a question about the $25 bundle, one month after the entry-level service launched,
Morrison cited a much lower uptake figure than the report’s: “the best estimates I’ve seen, Mr. Waugh,
are that about 4% of Canadians will go for it” (House of Commons Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage, 2016, time marks 0920–0925). He didn’t say how it bore on the job estimate, or
whether “4% of Canadians” meant all Canadians, households or television subscribers.

Comparing outcomes with the report’s reform paths
The prediction record (forecasters, quantities, paths by year, stated assumptions) was committed before any CRTC outcome series was downloaded. I then re-read the report’s two paths from the data
labels on its charts, checking every value against the totals in its text, and ran a design analysis by fakedata simulation before opening any CRTC outcome file (Gelman & Carlin, 2014; Gelman et al.,
2020). It asked, for each forecast quantity, whether the outcome could separate a world in which the
report’s impact occurred from one in which it didn’t, given that the baseline itself could be wrong.
For both revenue series it separated the two worlds in most simulations; for CPE it didn’t, so CPE
was set aside in advance as descriptive (appendix A).
Allowing for a wrong baseline is the hard part. In retrospective analysis of regulation, “developing
a credible counterfactual is the most demanding aspect”, and the baselines assumed beforehand “must
be revisited” to see whether outside factors changed the result (Morgenstern, 2018, p. 286). Here the
counterfactual is the report’s own baseline, itself a forecast of how television would have fared under
streaming without the reforms. So the check treats the baseline’s error as unknown and reports results
across a range of assumed scales for it. Kane (2026) varies the baseline period against which forecasts
are judged; here the baseline is the report’s own, and what varies is how wrong it’s assumed to be.
For each revenue series I estimate a forecast scale k. In logs, so that the forecast shortfall and the
baseline’s error are proportions of the baseline rather than dollar amounts, log Bt −log Yt = k δt +et ,

where Bt is the report’s baseline, Yt the outcome, δt the report’s forecast shortfall in logs (log Bt
minus the log of the reform path) and et the baseline’s error (positive when the baseline overstates
what would have happened without the decisions), modelled as a random walk from 2014. So k = 0
corresponds to the baseline and k = 1 to the reform path, with baseline error in either case. I write
k̂ for the estimate over 2016–2019, the four years before the pandemic.
Because the baseline’s error accumulates from year to year, k̂ rests on changes in the gap between
outcome and baseline rather than on its level: for specialty and pay revenue it’s positive although
that revenue stayed above the baseline in every year, because its excess over the baseline shrank, the
direction the reform path predicted.
The 95% interval for k reflects only the assumed error in the baseline, so its width depends on
the annual error scale σ, which is calibrated, not estimated from four years of outcomes, and treated
as known. The interval also assumes the forecast impact is proportional to the drifted baseline. Appendix A gives the estimator and what these assumptions cost: treating σ as uncertain widens the
intervals enough that the distributors’ interval includes zero, and an impact fixed in dollars lowers
coverage of k = 1 to 87% and 93% from 95%.
The scale k compares the outcome with the report’s two paths; it isn’t an estimate of the decisions’
causal effect, which would need a better counterfactual than the report’s own baseline. The value of
k for any other path is the estimate k̂ would take if revenue had followed that path exactly. The citedinput band is the range of such values for two paths built from the lowest and highest uptake estimates
the report cites (appendix A); it isn’t a probability interval.
The plan’s verdicts compare the 95% interval with the band, in this order: inconclusive if the
interval contains both zero and the band’s lower end; smaller if it lies wholly below the band; exceeded
if it lies wholly above; otherwise consistent.
The outcome series alone can’t identify how wrong the baseline was, so the plan fixed an external
check on σ: the report’s forecast of US Netflix subscribers, assumed unaffected by Canadian regulation, against Netflix’s reported count (appendix A). That check gives 1.3% a year, below the historical
volatility of either revenue series, and the pre-stated rule takes the larger of the two, so the designated
error scale is the historical volatility: 2.2% for specialty and pay revenue and 1.9% for distributors’
revenue.

4.1 Against the report’s paths
The CRTC series reproduce the report’s 2012–2014 values within 2% (CRTC, 2017, 2021), and
figure 1 shows each against the report’s two paths. Specialty and pay revenue stayed above the report’s
baseline, by between 0.8% and 4.6% from 2016 to 2019, and ran between 9.9% and 28.1% above the
reform path. Taken alone, the revenue fell 2.2% in nominal dollars from 2015 to 2019 and 8.9% after
inflation, while the report’s baseline was flat in nominal dollars over 2016–2019 (table B2).
For specialty and pay revenue, at the designated error scale of 2.2%, k̂ = 0.12, with a standard
error of 0.18. That’s 0.7 standard errors from k = 0 and 5.0 from k = 1, and the cited-input band’s

4.2 Uptake: the report’s assumption and the evidence

I found no later CRTC count.

4.3 Partial recalculations under assumed uptake paths
To ask what the report’s chain implies at other uptake levels, I rescale each year’s unbundling and
preponderance components by the assumed BYOP share over the report’s (Table 18) and hold the
exemption-order and closure dollar components fixed. Since the report values the exemption-order
and closure components partly from the other two (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. 220, 234), the
recalculation is partial. Its scenario coefficient k u is the value of k̂ that revenue following the recalculated path would give, and I call it compatible with the outcome when it lies inside the 95% interval for
k. Table 1 shows four uptake paths, and table B3 all seven. The paths were added after the outcome
data was opened, and the survey path uses a source outside the plan’s (table B8).
With uptake held at the CRTC’s June 2016 share, k u = 0.33 for specialty and pay revenue,
compatible with k̂ at the designated error scale (1.2 standard errors away), and 0.43 for distributors,
2.1 standard errors below k̂, a gap that falls below two under any calibration with a larger error scale
(section 4.5), with the CRTC series rescaled to the report’s 2014 level (1.9), and with the level offset
of section 4.6 (1.8). On the survey path, 1.6% in 2016 and 10% from 2017, k u = 0.76 for specialty
and pay revenue, 3.7 standard errors above k̂, though below the interval’s upper end at the largest
calibrated error scale, 4.7% (0.85), and 0.86 for distributors, 1.1 standard errors below. A linear rise
from 1.6% to 4% by 2019 gives 0.43 and 0.52, both inside their intervals. Among the paths tested, at
the designated error scale, that rise is the only one that keeps both series compatible with the partial
recalculation; larger error scales widen the set.

4.4 Distributors’ revenue by subscribers and revenue per subscriber
Splitting distributors’ revenue into subscribers and revenue per subscriber shows where their shortfall against the report’s baseline arose. The report’s components act on different factors: unbundling lowers revenue per subscriber and leaves subscriber numbers alone, while the exemption order
adds cord cutting, 1.9% of subscribers a year against 1.5% in the baseline (Nordicity & Miller, 2015,
paras. 204–205, 216, 220, Tables 5, 21). Rebuilding the report’s exemption-order component from
its stated cord cutting and cord shaving reproduces it to within about 1.1% in every year, so the reform
scenario’s split can be recovered (table B5).
The split is measured as changes from 2014, since in the report’s actual years the CRTC’s subscriber counts sit 1.7% to 2.1% below the report’s and its revenue per subscriber 0.5% to 0.7% above.
It assumes the two series stay comparable after 2014: the report’s subscriber series includes “nonreporting BDUs” (Fig. 17; BDUs, broadcasting distribution undertakings, is the CRTC’s term for
distributors), the CRTC’s cover broadcast years ending 31 August, and revenue per subscriber here
is total distributor revenue, which is broader than a package price.
From 2014 to 2019 the CRTC’s count of subscribers fell a further 3.9% relative to the report’s
baseline path, against 1.5% in its reform scenario, and revenue per subscriber fell 8.1% relative to it,
against 7.0%: it rose only 1.2% in nominal dollars, from $65.25 a month in 2014 to $66.05 in 2019
(6.8% lower after inflation), while the baseline path rose 10.1%; its underlying price assumption was
inflation plus one point, before an allowance for cord shaving (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 11,
paras. 151–152). In nominal terms distributors’ revenue fell 6.3% from 2014 to 2019, as subscribers
fell 7.5% and revenue per subscriber rose 1.2%; against the report’s rising baseline, missing growth in
revenue per subscriber accounts for about 68% of the shortfall, in logs.
With uptake held at the June 2016 share, the partial recalculation gives a per-subscriber shortfall
of 2.5%, and on the survey path 5.3%. Measured in logs from 2014, the share of the total revenue
shortfall that the recalculation leaves unexplained is 67% with uptake held at 1.6% and 44% on the
survey path.
The revenue split was added after the outcome data was opened, under a rule fixed before it was
computed (table B8); the comparisons with uptake held at 1.6% and on the survey path go beyond
that rule. It’s an accounting identity, and revenue per subscriber is computed from the same totals, so
the split locates the shortfall without observing prices or identifying causes. Subscriber losses exceed
those in both of the report’s paths compared here, and stagnant revenue per subscriber could reflect
pricing, cord shaving to smaller packages, or the reforms themselves.

5.1 Payments to Canadian channels
The report also assumed a path for what distributors pay Canadian channels. In its baseline the
monthly carriage fee per subscriber for Canadian specialty and pay services rises 1.6% from 2015 to
2019 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 14, p. 67), and under the decisions it falls below that path
as distributors pass on 75% of the retail loss attributed to Canadian services. What distributors paid
Canadian channels in affiliation payments rose from $3,014 million in 2015 to $3,125 million in
2019 while subscribers fell, so per subscriber it rose 10.5%, and the Canadian share of all affiliation
payments stayed near 87.8% (CRTC, 2019, Table U-T11). After inflation the total fell 3.5% and the
payment per subscriber rose 2.9%; the comparison with the report’s fee path needs no such adjustment, since that path is nominal too.
The report’s fee is the average monthly amount Canadian specialty, pay, pay-per-view and videoon-demand services earn per subscriber (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 180, Table 14); the CRTC
series is what distributors report paying all Canadian affiliates, divided by distributors’ subscribers.
The two differ in who reports them, in which services they cover and possibly in the subscriber base,
so their growth is compared, not their levels. With uptake near the CRTC’s 2016 share there would
have been little loss to pass on, and the comparison would mainly show that payments grew faster than
the report’s baseline assumed; on the survey path, the partial recalculation has fees falling relative to
the baseline path, and observed payments instead grew faster than it.
The aggregates can’t say what fees would have been without the decisions, or why affiliation payments per subscriber rose. One model predicts the direction observed. Simulating mandatory à la
carte in the US, Crawford and Yurukoglu (2012, p. 644) find equilibrium input costs about 103%
higher than under bundling once contracts between channels and distributors are renegotiated, the
opposite of the report’s assumption that unbundling lowers what distributors pay channels. The
rise here is consistent with that mechanism, though the aggregates can’t show it operated. Some of
the payments stay inside the integrated groups, and the report itself expected the exemption order’s
impact on those groups to be partly offset by their streaming and broadband revenue (Nordicity &
Miller, 2015, para. 227, p. 83). The CRTC’s wholesale code, which “governs the commercial relationship between BDUs and programmers” and was scheduled to take effect in January 2016 (Nordicity
& Miller, 2015, p. 72, n. 80, p. 73, n. 82), is in both of the report’s scenarios, so it can’t account for
the difference unless its effect exceeded what the report assumed.

5.3 Staff counts
For specialty and pay services and for distributors, the CRTC’s staff counts allow a descriptive comparison with the last link in the chain, from revenue to jobs (CRTC, 2017, 2021). The report gives its
forecast changes in direct employment by sector against a baseline whose employment path it doesn’t
publish, so the comparison is with the change from 2015 and with the 2012–2015 trend carried forward, a crude stand-in for that baseline. Specialty and pay services’ staff fell from 5,899 in 2015 to
4,403 in 2019, 1,282 below the trend, against a forecast shortfall in direct employment of 800 FTEs
by 2019. Distributors’ staff stayed near its 2012–2015 trend, 396 below it in 2018 and 1,834 above it
in 2019, against a forecast shortfall of 2,910 FTEs by 2019 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 22, p. 91).
The 2019 figure is a one-year jump that reverses the next year, which may be a reporting discontinuity.
Measured against these benchmarks, staff and revenue point in opposite directions in both sectors, which the report’s chain doesn’t imply: specialty and pay services cut staff below trend while
their revenue stayed above the report’s baseline, and distributors’ staffing stayed near trend while
their revenue fell below the baseline (annual series in table B6). The mismatch can’t test the report’s
employment prediction: staff counts aren’t the report’s modelled FTEs; the distributor series is labelled “Total Staff Count (FTE)” in the 2016 edition and “Total Staff Count” in the 2020 edition,
and the two differ by 302 in 2016; and a straight-line trend is a weak counterfactual. The direction
of the mismatch holds with the distributors’ 2018 figure in place of 2019.

Judged against the report rather than against outcomes, Morrison’s summary to the committee
departed from the report in three identifiable respects. It described an economy-wide employment

didn’t specify). His appearance also presented the losses as open to policy, citing the authors’ proposals to reduce them, and when a member cited officials’ evidence that funding for local production
had increased, he answered, “If it is right, that’s good news” (House of Commons Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage, 2016, time marks 0915–0920). What was lost between report and
committee was the numerical condition on which the headline rested, a particular case of the point
Manski (2011, p. 1) makes about policy analysis generally, where point predictions are common and
statements of uncertainty rare.

6.1 Limits of the check
The paper checks one forecast, and nothing here shows that stakeholder forecasts in general are biased;
Simpson (2014, p. 322) explains why even a run of overestimates needn’t show that. The verdict on
specialty and pay revenue rests on how wrong the report’s baseline is taken to be. I model that error as
a random walk calibrated in several ways, a simple model for stating the uncertainty, and a structural
break in viewing could exceed it. The intervals also treat the error scale as known, and they assume the
forecast impact applies proportionally to the drifted baseline; if it applied in dollars, as in the design
simulation, they’d cover k = 1 less often than 95% (appendix A).
The uptake evidence is thin: two CRTC counts from the spring and summer of 2016 and one
self-reported survey from 2017, which may not measure the report’s BYOP uptake, so the uptake
paths here illustrate the possibilities rather than bound them. The financial summaries used here
can’t separate closures of independent channels from continued operation under exemption, the aggregates can’t identify the pass-through rate, and staff counts are a different measure from the report’s
modelled employment. At its historical volatility, the design analysis gave CPE too little ability to distinguish the two simulated worlds for the planned comparison.
Five findings don’t depend on the model of the baseline’s error:
– The CRTC’s entry-level share in mid-2016 fell far short of the report’s 2016 BYOP assumption.
– Distributors’ subscriber numbers fell faster than in either of the report’s paths, if the two subscriber
series stay comparable.
– Affiliation payments to Canadian channels per subscriber grew faster than the report’s baseline
carriage fee.
– The CRTC’s count of specialty and pay revenue for 2015, a year with no forecast impact, was
already 2.5% above the report’s figure, against 0.8% in 2014.
– The testimony called an economy-wide estimate media jobs, presented the report’s 18% as a share
of current spending, and omitted the uptake assumption.
