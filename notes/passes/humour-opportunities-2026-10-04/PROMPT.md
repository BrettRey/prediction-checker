The paper is an Econ Journal Watch draft checking a commissioned forecast (Nordicity and Miller 2015) that the CRTC's 2015 television decisions would cost 15,130 jobs by 2020, put to a House of Commons committee in 2016 by Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting, against CRTC outcomes for 2016–2019. Econ Journal Watch publishes pointed, often dry critiques of economists' claims; its readers enjoy wit that sharpens an argument. The author's standard is "truthful, fair, and clear", and the paper names living people (Morrison; Peter Miller), so wit must never ridicule a person or imply bad faith the evidence doesn't show.

A previous pass looked only for problems with existing humour and found none. This pass is for opportunities. Follow this procedure (from the portfolio's pass registry):

HUMOUR. Brett's papers carry wit deliberately, and this half's main job is
finding opportunities for it, not policing it (Brett, 2026-10-04: "The humour
pass was not to look for problems but for opportunities"). Read for places
where wit would sharpen a point the paper already makes: a dry understatement,
a juxtaposition of a source's own words with what happened, an irony the
evidence already contains, a phrase a reader will remember and repeat. Propose
exact wording for each, and say what point it sharpens. Opportunities must come
from the content (never a joke imported for its own sake), must be fair to any
named person, and must fit the venue; "none found" needs the places looked at.

Then check every existing instance and every proposal against three questions:
does it help the point, does it fit the venue, does it undercut the register.
A joke that lands but makes a contested claim feel obvious is a metaphor
problem as much as a humour problem, so flag it for lakoffian-metaphor too.
Note that venue is part of the judgment, so this pass goes stale on a retarget
even when the prose has not moved.

House constraints on any proposal: no em dashes; no colon reveals ("The result: silence"); no paraprosdokians (a back half that subverts the front half for a punchline); no faux-insight setups; no "load-bearing"; contractions are fine; keep the scholarly register. Prefer wit that comes from the sources' own words set beside the outcomes, from understatement, or from an irony the numbers already contain. Every factual element of a proposal must already be in the paper.

Report: up to ten opportunities, ranked by how much each sharpens the point; for each, the location (section and quoted current text), the exact replacement text, the point it sharpens, and a one-line check against the three questions (helps the point? fits EJW? undercuts the register?). Then list the places you looked where you judged wit wrong for the moment (for example, passages about named people), and why.
