                                                       Abstract
               In April 2016 a House of Commons committee heard that new television rules from the
           Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission (CRTC) would cost 15,130
           media jobs by 2020. The figure came from a commissioned economic-impact study detailed
           enough to check. I fixed the forecast record before collecting outcomes and compared the study’s
           two scenarios with CRTC data for 2016–2019. The largest error was an input: the study as-
           sumed that 5% of subscribers would take the entry-level package in 2016, rising to 15%; the
           CRTC counted 1.6% by mid-2016. Specialty and pay channels’ revenue, on which most of the
           job figure depends, stayed above the study’s no-reform baseline, inconsistent with the forecast
           as made but consistent with the study’s model run at actual uptake. Distributors’ revenue was
           consistent with the forecast but fell more than that run predicts. Per-subscriber payments to Ca-
           nadian channels outgrew even the study’s no-reform path, and staffing didn’t track its revenue
           chain in either sector. The testimony presented figures that depended on that assumption as
           unconditional job losses, called an economy-wide estimate, more than half of it spin-off, media
           jobs, and cited a share of programming spending that the study’s own figures don’t reproduce.

    of Commons that the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission’s (CRTC’s)
    new rules for television would, “by 2020”, cost “some 15,130 media jobs”, “all of this as the direct
    result of Let’s Talk TV regulatory changes” (House of Commons Standing Committee on Canadian
    Heritage, 2016, time marks 0900–0905). The rules, the outcome of the CRTC’s Let’s Talk TV pro-
    ceeding, required cable, satellite and IPTV distributors to offer a $25 entry-level package and to sell

tions Commission [CRTC], 2015).
     The number came from an economic-impact study by the consultancy Nordicity and Peter Miller,
commissioned by Friends of Canadian Broadcasting and four other organizations in the sector (Nor-
dicity & Miller, 2015). It modelled what the rules would do to distributors’ and channels’ revenue,
to spending on Canadian programming, and, through input-output multipliers, to jobs and GDP
through 2020. Outcomes for those years are now public. I found no published check of the forecast
in the scholarly databases and web sources I searched, or in the authors’ later reports for the CRTC
(the searches are listed in the replication files).
     Economists have long compared what was predicted before a regulation with what happened after
it. Harrington et al. (1999, p. ii) found that ex ante estimates of direct costs exceeded actual costs for
12 of 25 environmental and occupational-safety rules and fell short for 6, with errors “driven by both
baseline and compliance issues”. A later set of case studies found “a slight tendency to overestimate
both costs and benefits” (Morgenstern, 2018, p. 285). Simpson (2014) warns that a preponderance
of overestimates need not show bias, since skewed cost distributions could produce one anyway; his
review notes that the few studies of industry’s own estimates found them above outcomes in most
cases (Simpson, 2014, pp. 319–320).
     The work I’ve cited concerns mainly regulators’ estimates of compliance costs and benefits. The
forecasts that interested parties put to legislators are a different object. They predict economy-wide
jobs and output through multipliers, they travel through testimony rather than regulatory filings, and
I know of no routine for revisiting them. In policy analysis generally, “Point predictions are common
and expressions of uncertainty are rare” (Manski, 2011, p. 4).
     The closest precedent is Kane (2026), who checked the enrolment forecasts made by universit-
ies and by Harvard’s expert during the litigation that ended in the US Supreme Court’s decision in
Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard. He reports results against more than one baseline, and he
separates the expert’s technical estimate from the forecasts the institutions put to the Court. I make
the same separation. The report’s technical claim is a conditional projection: if the rules took effect
and its assumptions held, revenue, programming spending and jobs would follow given paths. The
public argument is what the committee was told would happen.
     I fixed the forecast record before downloading any outcome data, and ran a simulation, before
opening the data, of whether they could tell a world with the forecast effect from one without it
(Gelman & Carlin, 2014; Gelman et al., 2020). The main outcome measure is a single number, k:
the observed shortfall below the report’s own no-reform baseline over 2016–2019, the years before
the pandemic, as a multiple of its forecast impact, so that k = 0 is no effect and k = 1 is the forecast.
     The largest single error was in an input. The report assumed that subscribers taking the entry-
level package with a few chosen channels would be 5% of all subscribers in 2016 and 15% from 2018,
a figure it chose from outside estimates of 10% to 35%. The CRTC counted 1.6% at the end of June
2016. Specialty and pay channels’ revenue, on which most of the job figure depends, stayed above
the report’s no-reform baseline: k = 0.12 (standard error 0.18; 95% interval −0.23 to 0.46).
     At the pre-stated calibration of the baseline’s error, that result is inconsistent with the report’s
forecast path at its assumed uptake. It’s consistent with a model-implied scenario, the report’s own
chain run on the uptake the CRTC counted, which gives k = 0.33; consistent isn’t confirmed, and
the scenario assumes uptake stayed near its 2016 level. Distributors’ revenue ran below the forecast

2   The decision, the forecast and the testimony
    In March 2015 the CRTC told television distributors to offer, by March 2016, an entry-level service
    “priced at no more than $25 (not including equipment) per month” (CRTC, 2015, para. 26). Every
    discretionary channel was to be available on its own or in small packages by March 2016, and in
    both forms by December 2016 (CRTC, 2015, para. 47). The policy came out of the Let’s Talk TV
    proceeding, at which, by one later account, “virtually all parties expressed concerns about potential
    impacts of unbundling” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. xxii, p. 5).
        In a report dated December 2015, and released by its authors’ later account in January 2016
    (Miller, 2022), Nordicity and Peter Miller published Canadian Television 2020. It was prepared for
    ACTRA, the Canadian Media Guild, the Directors Guild of Canada, Friends of Canadian Broadcast-
    ing and Unifor (Nordicity & Miller, 2015). It modelled two worlds from 2015 to 2020: a baseline
    with “technological and consumer behaviour changes, assuming that the pre-existing broadcasting
    regulatory regime remains”, and the same world with the CRTC’s decisions overlaid (Nordicity &
    Miller, 2015, para. ii, p. 1). Every headline number is the gap between the two.
        By 2020, the report concluded, the decisions were “likely to result in a loss of 15,130 FTEs of
    employment in the Canadian economy”, counted in full-time equivalents, along with $1,411.1 mil-
    lion of GDP. Of those jobs, 6,830 were directly in broadcasting and production and 8,300 in other
    sectors (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. xliv and Table 1, p. 12). Those figures sit at the end of a chain.
    Subscribers move to cheaper unbundled packages; distributors lose retail revenue and pass most of
    the loss to Canadian channels as lower wholesale fees; weaker channels close; spending on Canadian
    programming falls; and input-output multipliers turn the spending into jobs and GDP.
        The report states the assumptions that drive each link. Subscribers taking the entry-level service
    with a few chosen channels make up 5% of all subscribers in 2016, 10% in 2017 and 15% from 2018
    (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 18, p. 76). Distributors pass “75% of their retail revenue loss to
    3   How the forecast was checked                                                                         4


    Canadian programming services”, having attributed 86% of that loss to Canadian services (Nordicity
    & Miller, 2015, paras. 206–207, p. 77). By 2020, 10% of the large integrated companies’ Category
    A and B channels close, and 25% of the independents’ (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. 232–233,
    p. 85).
        By 2020 the model takes $970 million (23%) a year out of specialty and pay channels’ revenue
    and $858 million (9%) out of distributors’ revenue, against the baseline (Nordicity & Miller, 2015,
    paras. xxvi–xxvii).
    2.1 What the committee heard
    On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting, accompanied by Miller, put the
    report to the Commons Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage. Its key findings, he said, were
    that “by 2020, some 15,130 media jobs will be lost”, along with “a $400-million drop in Canadian
    program expenditures” that was “18% of what now exists”, “all of this as the direct result of Let’s Talk
    TV regulatory changes” (House of Commons Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage, 2016,
    time marks 0900–0905).
        Each phrase is stronger than the report. The 15,130 FTEs are economy-wide, and 8,300 of them
    are spin-off employment in other sectors; the 18% is a share of the report’s projected 2020 baseline,
    not of current spending; and the report itself splits its total into direct and spin-off impact. The figure
    also fails to reproduce. The report’s own baseline-scenario figures put 2020 Canadian programming
    expenditure at $3,155 million, so its $399 million reduction is 12.6% of baseline, not the 18% its text
    states (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 239, Figs. 8, 9, 43). Against what existed when Morrison
    spoke, the report’s own figure for 2015 spending, the reduction is 12.2%. None of the 12 shares
    I could build from the report’s figures, over 9 denominators and its two impact figures, gives 18%
    (table A1).
        Morrison also offered a forecast of his own, one month after the entry-level service launched:
    “the best estimates I’ve seen, Mr. Waugh, are that about 4% of Canadians will go for it” (House of
    Commons Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage, 2016, time marks 0920–0925).

3   How the forecast was checked
    The prediction record (forecasters, quantities, paths by year, stated assumptions) was committed be-
    fore any outcome series was downloaded. I then re-read the report’s two paths from the data labels
    on its charts, checking every value against the totals in its text, and ran a design analysis by fake-data
    simulation before opening any CRTC file (Gelman & Carlin, 2014; Gelman et al., 2020). It asked,
    for each forecast quantity, whether the outcome could separate a world in which the report’s impact
    occurred from one in which it didn’t, given that the baseline itself could be wrong.
         That last clause is the hard part. In retrospective analysis of regulation, “developing a credible
    counterfactual is the most demanding aspect”, and the baselines assumed beforehand “must be re-
    visited” to see whether outside factors changed the result (Morgenstern, 2018, p. 286). Here the
    counterfactual is the report’s own no-reform baseline, itself a forecast of how television would have
    fared under streaming without the reforms. So the check treats the baseline’s error as unknown, puts
    a range on it, and reports results across that range, much as Kane (2026) reports against more than
    one baseline.
         It depended on the quantity. With the baseline’s error set at the volatility of the report’s own
    2010–2014 data, specialty and pay revenue would be classified correctly in about 99% of simulated
    4   What happened                                                                                      5


    worlds and distributors’ revenue in about 86%. Canadian programming expenditure, whose histor-
    ical volatility was 9.1% a year, came out at 63%, so it was set aside in advance as descriptive.
         The outcome measure is a single number, k: the observed shortfall below the report’s baseline
    over 2016–2019, the four years before the pandemic, as a multiple of the report’s own forecast impact,
    so that k = 0 is no effect and k = 1 is the forecast. In logs, log Bt − log Yt = k δt + et , where Bt
    is the report’s baseline, Yt the outcome, δt the report’s forecast shortfall, and et the baseline’s error
    (positive when the baseline overstates what would have happened without the decisions), modelled
    as a random walk from 2014. The report’s paths and the CRTC series are both in nominal dollars,
    and since k compares them year by year, deflating both by the same price index leaves it unchanged.
         The 95% interval for k comes from generalized least squares under that model, and it reflects only
    the assumed error in the baseline, so its width depends on how large that error is taken to be. The
    scale of that error is calibrated from an outside comparison, described below, rather than estimated
    from four years of outcomes, and the CRTC’s administrative totals are treated as measured without
    error (appendix A; inputs in table A2).
         k compares the outcome with the report’s two forecast paths; it isn’t an estimate of the decisions’
    causal effect, which would need a better counterfactual than the report’s own baseline. The fore-
    casters’ own cited inputs (uptake between 10% and 35%, closures up to 2.5 times the assumed rate)
    define a band of k that counts as consistent with their forecast. An outcome below the forecast path
    was to be reported as the forecast exceeded, never as confirmed.
         How wrong the baseline was can’t be known from the outcomes, so the plan fixed an external
    check: the report’s forecast of US Netflix subscribers, which Canadian regulation couldn’t affect,
    against Netflix’s reported US paid memberships (Netflix, Inc., 2019). The report forecast 57.0 mil-
    lion for 2018 and Netflix reported 58.5 million, which by the pre-stated rule puts the error at 1.3% a
    year, below the historical volatility of both revenue series. The CRTC series reproduce the report’s
    2012–2014 values within 2% (CRTC, 2017, 2021).

5   The links in the chain
    The report’s own inputs can be checked without a counterfactual, and they show where its chain
    parted from events. The decisive one is uptake. The report took its 15% from outside estimates, the
    highest being Oliver Wyman’s finding that “as many as 35% of subscribers would be willing to switch”
    (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 201, p. 75). The CRTC counted 66,000 subscribers to the entry-
    level package five weeks after launch (CRTC, 2016a) and 177,000 by 30 June 2016 (CRTC, 2016b,
    para. 12), 0.6% and 1.6% of all subscribers, against the report’s 5% for 2016. I found no later count.
    Low uptake may partly reflect how the package was sold. The CRTC’s hearing of September 2016
    examined “the practices certain providers have adopted in offering” it (CRTC, 2016b, para. 5). Either
    way, the condition on which most of the forecast rested didn’t hold.
         The report also assumed a path for what distributors pay Canadian channels. In its no-reform
    baseline the monthly fee per subscriber for Canadian specialty and pay services rises 1.6% from 2015
    to 2019 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 14, p. 67), and under the decisions it falls below that path as
    distributors pass on 75% of their losses. What distributors paid Canadian channels rose from $3,014
    million in 2015 to $3,125 million in 2019 while subscribers fell, so per subscriber it rose 10.5%, and
    the Canadian share of all affiliation payments stayed near 87.8% (CRTC, 2019, Table U-T11). After
    inflation the total fell 3.5% and the payment per subscriber rose 2.9%; the comparison with the re-
    6   What the case shows                                                                                   9


    port’s fee path needs no such adjustment, since that path is nominal too. With so little uptake there
    was little loss to pass on, so this mainly shows that the baseline fee path was too pessimistic; still, fees
    ended above the report’s no-reform path, where the decisions scenario puts them below it.
         The aggregates don’t show why fees rose. Some of the payments stay inside the integrated groups,
    and the report itself expected those groups’ revenue impact to be partly offset (Nordicity & Miller,
    2015, para. 227, p. 83). The CRTC’s wholesale code, in force from January 2016 (Nordicity & Miller,
    2015, p. 73, n. 82), is in both of the report’s scenarios (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, p. 72, n. 80), so its
    fee path already allows for it.
         Closures are the one input that came out close to the report. Between 8% and 10% of the integ-
    rated companies’ Category A and B channels stopped operating by 2019–2020, against the report’s
    10%. For the independents the bounds run from 6% to 33%, because channels that left licensing for
    exemption can’t be told apart from channels that closed. Canadian programming expenditure, read
    only descriptively, stayed at or above the baseline path through 2019.
         The last link, from revenue to jobs, can be checked for two of the sectors, because the CRTC’s
    summaries report staff counts (CRTC, 2017, 2021). The report gives its direct FTE losses by sector
    against a baseline whose employment path it doesn’t publish, so the comparison is with the change
    from 2015 and with the 2012–2015 trend carried forward, a crude stand-in for that baseline. Chan-
    nels’ staff fell from 5,899 in 2015 to 4,403 in 2019, 1,282 below the trend, against a forecast direct
    loss of 800 by 2019. Distributors’ staff stayed near its pre-2015 trend, 396 below it in 2018 and 1,834
    above it in 2019, against a forecast direct loss of 2,910 by 2019 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 22,
    p. 91). The 2019 figure is a one-year jump that reverses the next year, which may be a reporting
    discontinuity.
         Observed staffing doesn’t track the revenue chain in either sector: channels cut staff while their
    revenue held, and distributors’ staffing stayed near trend while their revenue fell (annual series in
    table A5). It’s a descriptive mismatch, not a test of the model’s employment prediction. The counts
    aren’t the report’s modelled full-time equivalents, the distributor series changes from an FTE count
    to a staff count between releases (the two differ by 302 in 2016), and a straight-line trend is a weak
    counterfactual. The direction of the mismatch holds with the distributors’ 2018 figure in place of
    2019.

6   What the case shows
    Read as a conditional projection, the report did better than its headline. Its largest error was an input,
    the share of subscribers who would take the entry-level package, which is the kind of quantity error
    Harrington et al. (1999, p. ii) found behind misses in regulatory cost estimates. Run on the uptake
    that occurred, as a model-implied scenario, its prediction for channel revenue is consistent with the
    outcome at the pre-stated calibration, which falls short of confirming it. The outcomes depart from
    it in the parts uptake doesn’t touch. Distributors lost more revenue than the model gives at that
    uptake (by 2.1 standard errors at the pre-stated calibration), the fees they paid Canadian channels
    ended above even the report’s no-reform path, and observed staffing doesn’t track the revenue chain.
         The jobs figure put to Parliament rests on that chain, and the report divides it two ways (table A6;
    Nordicity and Miller, 2015, paras. 246, 248, 250, Tables 22–23, pp. 91–92). By kind of effect, 6,830
    of the 15,130 FTEs are direct jobs in broadcasting and production and 8,300 are spin-off jobs in other
    sectors. By the sector whose losses give rise to them, 7,180 are attributed to independent production
6   What the case shows                                                                                 10


(2,830 direct, 4,350 spin-off) and 7,950 to broadcasting (4,000 direct, 3,950 spin-off). The 10,060
(66%) attributed to specialty and pay services (2,880) and to production depend in the model on
Canadian channels’ revenue and programming spending.
     The staff counts don’t track that chain: channels shed more staff than the forecast’s direct FTE
loss although their revenue held, and distributors’ staffing stayed near trend although their revenue
fell. Channel employment fell, then, but not by the route the model describes, and distributor staff-
ing didn’t show the fall the model tied to their revenue loss; both are descriptive comparisons with
unlike measures. Spin-off employment has no observable counterpart, and production employment
remains to be checked.
     The public argument is where the forecast failed outright. The testimony presented an economy-
wide estimate, more than half of it spin-off employment, as media jobs that would be lost, as the
direct result of the policy, and it quoted a share of programming spending that the report’s own
figures don’t support. It also didn’t say that the figures rested on an uptake assumption. In the same
appearance Morrison estimated uptake at 4%, a level at which the report’s own chain gives channel
losses of about 0.40 of the forecast (his figure, like the CRTC’s counts, refers to the entry-level package
itself, not quite the report’s measure). On the way from report to committee, a conditional projection
with stated assumptions became a point prediction without them. It’s a particular case of the point
Manski (2011, p. 4) makes about policy analysis generally, where point predictions are common and
statements of uncertainty rare.
     The report deserves credit for what made this check possible. It stated its assumptions link by
link, published annual paths for both scenarios, cited the range of outside estimates it chose among,
and gave the figures behind its headline. A forecast of a single number would have left nothing to
check but the number. The useful lesson for those who commission or hear such forecasts is in that
contrast. Intermediate predictions that public data can test, an uptake share or a payment flow in the
second year, let a forecast be held to account before its end date, and a statement of how wrong the
baseline could be shows in advance how much the headline depends on it.
6.1 Limits of the check
The paper checks one forecast, and nothing here shows that stakeholder forecasts in general are biased;
Simpson (2014) explains why even a run of overestimates needn’t show that. The verdict on channel
revenue rests on how wrong the report’s technology-only baseline is taken to be. I model that error
as a random walk calibrated in several ways, the simplest model that lets the uncertainty be stated,
and a structural break in viewing could exceed it. Entry-level uptake is known only for 2016, so the
conditional reading assumes it stayed near that level; if it rose toward the report’s assumption, that
reading weakens. Closures of independent channels can’t be separated from moves to exemption, the
aggregates show that the pass-through didn’t appear without showing why, and the staff counts are
a different measure from the report’s modelled employment. Canadian programming expenditure
moved too much from year to year, before the reforms, to test the forecast on that measure.
    Four findings don’t depend on the model of the baseline’s error at all: uptake fell far short of the
report’s assumption; payments to Canadian channels per subscriber ended above the report’s own
no-reform path; the CRTC’s count of specialty and pay revenue for 2015, a year with no forecast
impact, was already 2.5% above the report’s figure, against 0.8% in 2014; and the testimony stated
more than the report did.

   Design analysis
   Before any outcome data was opened, each quantity was simulated 20,000 times (seed 20261003)
   in two worlds: without the forecast effect, Yt = Bt e−et , so that yt = et and k = 0; and with it,
   Yt = Bt e−et − It , the forecast impact applied in levels to the drifted baseline. The second gives
   yt = δt + et (k = 1) up to a term of order et It /(Bt − It ). The simulation code writes the drift
   with the opposite sign, Bt eet ; since the errors are symmetric about zero, the two describe the same
   distribution. Each simulated path was assigned to whichever of the report’s two paths gives it the
   higher Gaussian likelihood under Σ. “Classified correctly” is the share of paths assigned to the world
   that generated them; the figures in the text are the smaller of the two worlds’ shares, at the volatility
   of the report’s own 2010–2014 data.
