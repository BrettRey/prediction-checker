Checking a forecast put to Parliament: unbundling
Canadian television, 2016–2019
Brett Reynolds1
October 4, 2026

Abstract
I check a forecast put to a Canadian parliamentary committee: a commissioned study’s estimate
that new television rules would cost 15,130 jobs by 2020. Under a plan fixed before collecting outcomes,
I compare its intermediate predictions with regulator data for 2016–2019; neither the job total nor the
rules’ causal effect is tested. At the error scale the plan set for the study’s baseline, the channel revenue on which most of the job figure depends lost less ground against that baseline than the study’s
model implies at any package uptake it cited, and distributors’ revenue was consistent with the forecast, though too imprecisely to tell a shortfall half the forecast’s size from one twice as large. Under the
largest error scales some calibrations give, both verdicts become inconclusive. How far lower uptake
reconciles the channel result with the study’s model depends on uptake after mid-2016, when the regulator counted 1.6% of subscribers on the new entry-level package, against the study’s 5% taking it with
chosen channels; a 2017 survey outside the plan’s sources put adoption near the study’s figure for that
year. The testimony called an economy-wide estimate media jobs and omitted the uptake assumption.

Economists have long compared what was predicted before a regulation with what happened
after it. Harrington et al. (2000, 297) found that ex ante estimates of total direct costs exceeded
actual costs for 14 of 28 environmental and occupational-safety rules and fell short for 3, with the
quantity errors “driven both by difficulties in determining the baseline and by incomplete compliance”, and a later set of case studies suggested “a slight tendency to overestimate both costs and
benefits” (Morgenstern 2018, 285). Simpson (2014, 322) warns that a preponderance of overestiECON JOURNAL WATCH

mates need not show bias, since skewed cost distributions could produce one anyway; his review
notes that the few studies of industry’s own estimates found them above outcomes in most cases
(319–321).
That work concerns mainly regulators’ estimates of compliance costs and benefits. This case is
a different kind of forecast: an economy-wide estimate of jobs and output, made for stakeholder
organizations and presented to legislators in testimony. Such estimates have a critical literature
of their own. Crompton (2006, 67) documents how the job and output totals of commissioned
economic-impact studies are inflated through multipliers and generous assumptions about which
spending is new, and independent studies of sports facilities have found no statistically significant
positive association between facility construction and local development (Siegfried and Zimbalist 2000, 103). This paper doesn’t test the report’s multipliers; it checks the predictions they were
applied to. In policy analysis generally, “Point predictions are common and expressions of uncertainty are rare” (Manski 2011, 1).
A close precedent is Kane (2026), who checked the admissions and enrolment forecasts
made by universities and by Harvard’s expert during the litigation that ended in the US Supreme
Court’s decision in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard. He separates the expert’s technical
estimate from the forecasts the institutions put to the Court, and I make the same separation.
The report’s technical claim is a conditional forecast, which its authors offered as “the most
plausible outlooks possible given available information” (Nordicity and Miller 2015, para. iii): if
the rules took effect and its assumptions held, revenue, programming spending and jobs would
follow given paths. The public argument is what the committee was told.
For the technical claim, I fixed the forecast record before downloading any CRTC outcome
file, and ran a simulation, before opening those files, of whether they could tell a world with the
forecast effect from one without it (Gelman and Carlin 2014; Gelman et al. 2020). The main
outcome measure is a forecast scale 𝑘 for each revenue series, estimated over 2016–2019, the years
before the pandemic: 𝑘 = 0 corresponds to the report’s baseline and 𝑘 = 1 to its forecast, under

The Forecast and Its Presentation to Parliament
The forecast
In March 2015 the CRTC told television distributors to offer, by March 2016, an entry-level service
“priced at no more than $25 (not including equipment) per month” (CRTC 2015, para. 26). Every
discretionary channel was to be available on its own or in small packages by March 2016, and in
both forms by December 2016 (CRTC 2015, para. 47). The policy came out of the Let’s Talk
TV proceeding, at which, by one later account, “virtually all parties expressed concerns about
potential impacts of unbundling” (Nordicity and Miller 2015, para. xxii, p. 5).
Nordicity and Peter Miller’s Canadian Television 2020, dated December 2015, was prepared
for ACTRA (the Alliance of Canadian Cinema, Television and Radio Artists), the Canadian
Media Guild, the Directors Guild of Canada, Friends of Canadian Broadcasting and Unifor. It
modelled two worlds from 2015 to 2020: a baseline with “technological and consumer behaviour
changes, assuming that the pre-existing broadcasting regulatory regime remains”, and the same
world with the CRTC’s decisions overlaid (Nordicity and Miller 2015, para. ii, p. 1). I call these the
report’s baseline and its reform scenario; the forecast is the gap between them, and a quantity’s

path under the reform scenario is its reform path. A third scenario, with the decisions modified,
isn’t checked here.
By 2020, the report concluded, the decisions were “likely to result in a loss of 15,130 FTEs
of employment in the Canadian economy”, counted in full-time equivalents (FTEs), along with
$1,411.1 million of GDP. Of those jobs, 6,830 were directly in broadcasting and production and
8,300 were spin-off employment, the indirect and induced employment in other sectors (Nordicity and Miller 2015, para. xliv and Table 1 with its note, p. 12). The report also divides the total
by the sector whose losses give rise to it: 7,180 to independent production (2,830 direct, 4,350
spin-off) and 7,950 to broadcasting (4,000 direct, 3,950 spin-off), as table B7 sets out (Nordicity
and Miller 2015, paras. 246, 248, 250; Tables 22–23, pp. 90–92). The 10,060 (66%) attributed to
production and, within broadcasting, to specialty and pay services depend in the model on those
services’ revenue and on spending on Canadian programming, which is why specialty and pay
revenue is the main test below.
The job and GDP figures sit at the end of a chain. In the report’s model, subscribers move to
cheaper unbundled packages; distributors lose retail revenue and pass most of the loss to Canadian channels as lower wholesale fees; weaker channels close; spending on Canadian programming falls; and the authors apply input-output multipliers to the projected spending reductions
to estimate employment and GDP losses. The report links these estimates to reductions in broadcasting revenue and production financing (Nordicity and Miller 2015, para. 245), and I found no
offset in it for the spending that subscribers who pay less redirect elsewhere. The report states the
assumptions that drive each link. Subscribers taking the entry-level package with chosen channels, which the report calls Build Your Own Package (BYOP), make up 5% of all subscribers in
2016, 10% in 2017 and 15% from 2018 (Nordicity and Miller 2015, para. 202, Table 18, p. 76); I call
this the report’s BYOP uptake. Distributors attribute 86% of the retail revenue they lose to Canadian services and pass 75% of that part on to them as lower wholesale fees (Nordicity and Miller
2015, paras. 206–207, p. 77). By 2020, 10% of the Category A and B specialty channels owned by

The report divides the revenue shortfall into four components: unbundling; the change from
requiring a preponderance of Canadian channels among those each subscriber receives to among
those offered, with the end of access rules, which it models as Canadian channels’ share of BYOP
subscribers’ wholesale fees falling from 86% to 50% by 2020 (Nordicity and Miller 2015, para. 212);
an exemption order under which streaming services such as Crave TV and Shomi, until then
offered only to TV subscribers, could compete for all viewers, which it expected to “materially
increase cord cutting and cord shaving” (Nordicity and Miller 2015, para. xxiii), that is, cancellations and downgrades of TV subscriptions; and channel closures (Nordicity and Miller 2015,
Figs. 2–3).
The modelled revenue losses from unbundling and the preponderance change act through
BYOP subscribers and so scale with uptake; the report sets the losses from the exemption order
and closures separately, though it calculates them partly from the other two (Nordicity and Miller
2015, paras. 220, 234).

What the committee heard
On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting, accompanied by Miller, put
the report to the Commons Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage. Its key findings, he
said, were that “by 2020, some 15,130 media jobs will be lost”, along with “a $400-million drop
in Canadian program expenditures” that was “18% of what now exists”, “all of this as the direct
result of Let’s Talk TV regulatory changes”. He added that the authors had “advanced proposals
to reduce the negative impact of the CRTC’s decisions by as much as 75%” (House of Commons
Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage 2016, time marks 0900–0905).
Two of these phrases depart from the report. The testimony’s “media jobs” have a majority outside the media. The report’s 15,130 FTEs are economy-wide, including 8,300 in spin-off
employment in other sectors. The report describes its 18% as a share of projected 2020 baseline
spending, not of current spending. The $400 million rounds the report’s $399 million, and “direct result” attributes the losses to the decisions, as the report does, though “will be lost” is firmer
than the report’s “likely”. The 18% itself is the report’s, though its figures don’t reproduce it: its
baseline puts 2020 CPE at $3,155 million, so its $399 million reduction is 12.6% of baseline, not
the 18% its text states (Nordicity and Miller 2015, para. 239; Figs. 8, 9, 43). Against the report’s
own forecast for 2015, the reduction is 12.2%, and against what existed when Morrison spoke,
the CRTC’s count of 2015 spending ($3,286 million), it’s 12.1% (CRTC 2017). The reported 18%
proves easier to quote than to reproduce. None of the 12 shares tested, built from the report’s
figures over 9 denominators and its two impact figures, gives 18% (table B1).
Responding to a question about the $25 bundle, one month after the entry-level service
launched, Morrison cited a much lower uptake figure than the report’s: “the best estimates I’ve
seen, Mr. Waugh, are that about 4% of Canadians will go for it” (House of Commons Standing
Committee on Canadian Heritage 2016, time marks 0920–0925). He didn’t say whether “4% of
Canadians” meant all Canadians, households or television subscribers, and neither this answer

Comparing Outcomes with the Report’s Reform Paths
The prediction record (forecasters, quantities, paths by year, stated assumptions) was committed
before any CRTC outcome series was downloaded. I then re-read the report’s two paths from the
data labels on its charts, checking every value against the totals in its text, and ran a design analysis by fake-data simulation before opening any CRTC outcome file (Gelman and Carlin 2014;
Gelman et al. 2020). It asked, for each forecast quantity, whether the outcome could separate a
world in which the report’s impact occurred from one in which it didn’t, given that the baseline
itself could be wrong. It separated the two worlds well for both revenue series and only weakly
for CPE, so CPE was set aside in advance as descriptive (appendix A).
Allowing for a wrong baseline is the hard part. In retrospective analysis of regulation, “developing a credible counterfactual is the most demanding aspect”, and the baselines assumed beforehand “must be revisited” to see whether outside factors changed the result (Morgenstern 2018,
286). Here the counterfactual is the report’s own baseline, itself a forecast of how television would
have fared under streaming without the reforms. So the check treats the baseline’s error as unknown and reports results across a range of assumed scales for it. Kane (2026) varies the baseline
period against which forecasts are judged; here the baseline is the report’s own, and what varies is
how wrong it’s assumed to be.
For each revenue series I estimate a forecast scale 𝑘 . In logs, so that the forecast shortfall and
the baseline’s error are relative differences rather than dollar amounts, log 𝐵 𝑡 − log 𝑌𝑡 = 𝑘 𝛿𝑡 + 𝑒𝑡 ,
where 𝐵 𝑡 is the report’s baseline, 𝑌𝑡 the outcome, 𝛿𝑡 the report’s forecast shortfall in logs (log 𝐵 𝑡
minus the log of the reform path) and 𝑒𝑡 the baseline’s error (positive when the baseline overstates
what would have happened without the decisions), modelled as a random walk from 2014. So

𝑘 = 0 corresponds to the baseline and 𝑘 = 1 to the reform path, with baseline error in either case.

I write 𝑘ˆ for the estimate over 2016–2019, the four years before the pandemic.
Because the baseline’s error accumulates from year to year, 𝑘ˆ rests on changes in the gap between outcome and baseline rather than on its level: for specialty and pay revenue it’s positive
although that revenue stayed above the baseline in every year, because its excess over the baseline
shrank, the direction the reform path predicted.
The 95% interval for 𝑘 reflects only the assumed error in the baseline, so its width depends
on the annual error scale 𝜎 , which is calibrated, not estimated from four years of outcomes, and
treated as known. The interval also assumes the forecast impact is proportional to the drifted
baseline. I also give 50% intervals, which the true value should be as likely to fall inside as outside
(Gelman and Hill 2007, 18); the verdicts use the 95% interval. Appendix A gives the estimator
and what these assumptions cost: in its illustration of uncertainty in 𝜎 , the distributors’ interval
includes zero, and an impact fixed in dollars lowers coverage of 𝑘 = 1 to 87% and 93% from 95%.
The scale 𝑘 compares the outcome with the report’s two paths; it isn’t an estimate of the
decisions’ causal effect, which would need a better counterfactual than the report’s own baseline.
The value of 𝑘 for any other path is the estimate 𝑘ˆ would take if revenue had followed that path
exactly. The cited-input band is the range of such values for two paths built from the lowest and
highest uptake estimates the report cites (appendix A); it isn’t a probability interval.
The plan’s verdicts compare the 95% interval with the band, in this order: inconclusive if the
interval contains both zero and the band’s lower end; smaller if it lies wholly below the band;
exceeded if it lies wholly above; otherwise consistent.
The outcome series alone can’t identify how wrong the baseline was, so the plan fixed an external check on 𝜎 : the report’s forecast of US Netflix subscribers, assumed unaffected by Canadian
regulation, against Netflix’s reported count (appendix A). That check gives 1.3% a year, below the
historical volatility of either revenue series, and the pre-stated rule takes the larger of the two, so
the designated error scale is the historical volatility: 2.2% for specialty and pay revenue and 1.9% for

Revenue Outcomes and Their Sensitivity to Baseline Error
Against the report’s paths
The CRTC series reproduce the report’s 2012–2014 values within 2% (CRTC 2017; 2021), and
figure 1 shows each against the report’s two paths. Specialty and pay revenue stayed above the
report’s baseline, by between 0.8% and 4.6% from 2016 to 2019, and ran between 9.9% and 28.1%
above the reform path. Taken alone, the revenue fell 2.2% in nominal dollars from 2015 to 2019
and 8.9% after inflation, while the report’s baseline was flat in nominal dollars over 2016–2019
(table B2).
For specialty and pay revenue, at the designated error scale of 2.2%, 𝑘ˆ = 0.12, with a standard
error of 0.18. That’s 0.7 standard errors from 𝑘 = 0 and 5.0 from 𝑘 = 1, and the cited-input band’s
lower end, 0.75, is 3.6 standard errors away. Under the pre-stated rule the verdict is smaller: the
95% interval, −0.23 to 0.46 (50%: 0.00 to 0.24), lies wholly below the band of 0.75 to 2.54, so at
this scale the outcome is incompatible with the report’s chain at any uptake it cited, with its other
components as the report had them.

Uptake: the report’s assumption and the evidence
The report took its 15% for 2018 onward from outside estimates, the highest being Oliver Wyman’s
finding that “as many as 35% of subscribers would be willing to switch to a skinny-basic/BYOP
option” (Nordicity and Miller 2015, para. 201, p. 75). The CRTC counted more than 66,000
subscribers to the entry-level package five weeks after launch (CRTC 2016a) and 177,000 by 30
June 2016 (CRTC 2016d, para. 12), about 0.6% and 1.6% of the 2016 subscriber total. These count
subscribers to the entry-level package with or without added channels, a different measure from
the report’s BYOP uptake, taken before every channel had to be offered singly as well as in small
packages; I found no later CRTC count.

The split is measured as changes from 2014, since in the report’s actual years the CRTC’s
subscriber counts sit 1.7% to 2.1% below the report’s and its revenue per subscriber 0.5% to 0.7%
above. It assumes the two series stay comparable after 2014: the report’s subscriber series includes
“non-reporting BDUs” (Fig. 17; BDUs, broadcasting distribution undertakings, is the CRTC’s
term for distributors), the CRTC’s series cover broadcast years ending 31 August, and revenue
per subscriber here is total distributor revenue, which is broader than a package price.
From 2014 to 2019 the CRTC’s count of subscribers fell a further 3.9% relative to the report’s
baseline path, against 1.5% in its reform scenario, and revenue per subscriber fell 8.1% relative to it,

A check of the statistical error model, added after the outcome data was opened, finds the specialty

Limits of the check
The paper checks one forecast, and nothing here shows that stakeholder forecasts in general are
biased; Simpson (2014, 322) explains why even a run of overestimates needn’t show that. The verdict on specialty and pay revenue rests on how wrong the report’s baseline is taken to be. I model
that error as a random walk, a simple model for stating the uncertainty, with its scale calibrated in
several ways from proxies; a structural break in viewing could exceed it. The pre-stated intervals
also treat the error scale as known (the post hoc integrated checks relax that), and they assume
the forecast impact applies proportionally to the drifted baseline; if it applied in dollars, as in the
design simulation, they’d cover 𝑘 = 1 less often than 95% (appendix A).
The uptake evidence is thin: two CRTC counts from the spring and summer of 2016 and one
self-reported survey from 2017, which may not measure the report’s BYOP uptake, so the uptake
paths here illustrate the possibilities rather than bound them. The financial summaries used here
can’t separate closures of independent channels from continued operation under exemption, the

aggregates can’t identify the pass-through rate, and staff counts are a different measure from the
report’s modelled employment. At its historical volatility, the design analysis gave CPE too little
ability to distinguish the two simulated worlds for the planned comparison.
Five findings don’t depend on the model of the baseline’s error:
• The CRTC’s entry-level share in mid-2016 fell far short of the report’s 2016 BYOP assumption.
• Distributors’ subscriber numbers fell faster than in either of the report’s paths, if the two
subscriber series stay comparable.
• Affiliation payments to Canadian channels per subscriber grew faster than the report’s
baseline carriage fee, in a comparison of two differently defined measures.
• The CRTC’s count of specialty and pay revenue for 2015, a year with no forecast impact,
was already 2.5% above the report’s figure, against 0.8% in 2014.
• The testimony called an economy-wide estimate media jobs, presented the report’s 18%
as a share of current spending, and omitted the uptake assumption.
One feature of this case parallels what Kane (2026, 486) found in litigation, where an expert’s
forecast was stated “in units that correspond to no publicly reported statistic”. The report’s main
input, the share of subscribers taking BYOP with chosen channels, corresponds to no figure the
CRTC publishes: the regulator counted subscribers to the entry-level package twice in 2016, and
I found no count after that, which is why this paper gives more than one reading of uptake. A
forecast put to legislators can be checked link by link only if its intermediate predictions are stated
in quantities someone goes on measuring, and a committee can weigh its headline only if the
conditions on it arrive with it. A committee hearing such a forecast can ask for both.
