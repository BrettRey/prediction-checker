                 Canadian television, 2016–2019
                                         Brett Reynolds *
                             Humber Polytechnic & University of Toronto

                                                          Abstract
               I check a forecast put to a Canadian parliamentary committee: a commissioned study’s es-
           timate that new television rules would cost 15,130 jobs by 2020. Under a plan fixed before
           collecting outcomes, I compare its intermediate predictions with regulator data for 2016–2019;
           neither the job total nor the rules’ causal effect is tested. At the plan’s error scale, the channel rev-
           enue on which most of the job figure depends lost less ground against the study’s baseline than
           any uptake estimate the study cited implies, and distributors’ revenue was consistent with the
           forecast. How far the uptake assumption explains this depends on uptake after mid-2016, when
           the regulator counted 1.6% of subscribers on the new entry-level package, against the study’s 5%
           taking it with chosen channels; a 2017 survey outside the plan’s sources put adoption near the
           study’s figure for that year. The testimony called an economy-wide estimate media jobs and
           omitted the uptake assumption.

1   Introduction
    On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting told a committee of the House
    of Commons that the Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunications Commission’s (CRTC’s)
    new rules for television would, “by 2020”, cost “some 15,130 media jobs”, “all of this as the direct
    result of Let’s Talk TV regulatory changes” (House of Commons Standing Committee on Canadian
    Heritage, 2016, time marks 0900–0905). The rules, the outcome of the CRTC’s Let’s Talk TV pro-
    ceeding, required cable, satellite and IPTV distributors to offer a $25 entry-level package and to sell
    discretionary channels singly or in small bundles (Canadian Radio-television and Telecommunica-
    tions Commission [CRTC], 2015).

     The number came from an economic-impact study by the consultancy Nordicity and Peter Miller,
commissioned by Friends of Canadian Broadcasting and four other organizations in the sector; the re-
port attributes its analysis and conclusions to the authors, not necessarily the commissioning groups
(Nordicity & Miller, 2015). It modelled what the rules would do to distributors’ and channels’ rev-
enue, to spending on Canadian programming, and, through input-output multipliers, to jobs and
GDP through 2020, stating its assumptions link by link. CRTC series on revenue, programming
spending and staff are now available for those years. My recorded searches of scholarly databases and
the web found no published comparison of the forecast’s numerical paths with outcomes (the quer-
ies are in the replication files). Miller’s later work for the CRTC discusses what the decisions led to,
and acknowledges an error in a separate prediction of station closures (Miller, 2022, n. 88, n. 196).
     This paper compares the report’s intermediate predictions (the revenue of specialty and pay ser-
vices and of distributors, uptake, payments, closures and staff) with what happened through 2019,
and compares the report with what the committee was told. It tests neither the total employment
forecast nor the decisions’ causal effect. In the comparisons fixed in advance, at the error scales the
plan designated, specialty and pay revenue, on which most of the job figure depends, stayed above the
report’s baseline and lost less ground against it than any uptake estimate the report cited implies; dis-
tributors’ revenue was consistent with the forecast. The report’s main input, the share of subscribers
taking an entry-level package with chosen channels, was far above the CRTC’s mid-2016 count of
entry-level subscribers, though a 2017 survey, outside the sources the plan accepted, suggests starter-
package adoption later came close to the report’s figure. Among the uptake paths tested, at those
scales, only a low one keeps both revenue series compatible with a partial recalculation of the report’s
chain. The testimony described the report’s figures in two ways the report doesn’t support and omit-
ted the uptake assumption.
     Economists have long compared what was predicted before a regulation with what happened
after it. Harrington et al. (1999, p. ii) found that ex ante estimates of direct costs exceeded actual
costs for 12 of 25 environmental and occupational-safety rules and fell short for 6, with the errors in
quantities “driven by both baseline and compliance issues”, and a later set of case studies suggested
“a slight tendency to overestimate both costs and benefits” (Morgenstern, 2018, p. 285). Simpson
(2014) warns that a preponderance of overestimates need not show bias, since skewed cost distribu-
tions could produce one anyway; his review notes that the few studies of industry’s own estimates
found them above outcomes in most cases (Simpson, 2014, pp. 319–320).
     That work concerns mainly regulators’ estimates of compliance costs and benefits. This case is
a different kind of forecast: an economy-wide estimate of jobs and output, made for stakeholder or-
ganizations and presented to legislators in testimony. In policy analysis generally, “Point predictions
are common and expressions of uncertainty are rare” (Manski, 2011, p. 1).
     A close precedent is Kane (2026), who checked the admissions and enrolment forecasts made
by universities and by Harvard’s expert during the litigation that ended in the US Supreme Court’s
decision in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard. He separates the expert’s technical estimate
from the forecasts the institutions put to the Court, and I make the same separation. The report’s
technical claim is a conditional forecast, which its authors offered as “the most plausible outlooks
possible given available information” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. iii): if the rules took effect and
its assumptions held, revenue, programming spending and jobs would follow given paths. The public
argument is what the committee was told.

2   The forecast and its presentation to Parliament
    In March 2015 the CRTC told television distributors to offer, by March 2016, an entry-level service
    “priced at no more than $25 (not including equipment) per month” (CRTC, 2015, para. 26). Every
    discretionary channel was to be available on its own or in small packages by March 2016, and in
    both forms by December 2016 (CRTC, 2015, para. 47). The policy came out of the Let’s Talk TV
    proceeding, at which, by one later account, “virtually all parties expressed concerns about potential
    impacts of unbundling” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. xxii, p. 5).
        Nordicity and Peter Miller’s Canadian Television 2020, dated December 2015, was prepared for
    ACTRA, the Canadian Media Guild, the Directors Guild of Canada, Friends of Canadian Broadcast-
    ing and Unifor (Nordicity & Miller, 2015). It modelled two worlds from 2015 to 2020: a baseline
    with “technological and consumer behaviour changes, assuming that the pre-existing broadcasting
    regulatory regime remains”, and the same world with the CRTC’s decisions overlaid (Nordicity &
    Miller, 2015, para. ii, p. 1). I call these the report’s baseline and its reform scenario; the forecast is
    the gap between them, and a quantity’s path under the reform scenario is its reform path. A third
    scenario, with the decisions modified, isn’t checked here.
        By 2020, the report concluded, the decisions were “likely to result in a loss of 15,130 FTEs of em-
    ployment in the Canadian economy”, counted in full-time equivalents (FTEs), along with $1,411.1
    million of GDP. Of those jobs, 6,830 were directly in broadcasting and production and 8,300 were
    spin-off employment, the indirect and induced employment in other sectors (Nordicity & Miller,
    2015, para. xliv and Table 1 with its note, p. 12). The report also divides the total by the sector whose
    losses give rise to it: 7,180 to independent production (2,830 direct, 4,350 spin-off) and 7,950 to
    broadcasting (4,000 direct, 3,950 spin-off) (table A7; Nordicity and Miller, 2015, paras. 246, 248,
    250, Tables 22–23, pp. 90–92). The 10,060 (66%) attributed to specialty and pay services and to pro-
    duction depend in the model on those services’ revenue and on spending on Canadian programming,
    which is why specialty and pay revenue is the main test below.
        Those figures sit at the end of a chain. Subscribers move to cheaper unbundled packages; dis-
    tributors lose retail revenue and pass most of the loss to Canadian channels as lower wholesale fees;
    weaker channels close; spending on Canadian programming falls; and input-output multipliers turn
    the spending into jobs and GDP. The report states the assumptions that drive each link. Subscribers
    taking the entry-level package with chosen channels, which the report calls Build Your Own Pack-
    3   Comparing outcomes with the report’s reform paths                                                      4


    age (BYOP), make up 5% of all subscribers in 2016, 10% in 2017 and 15% from 2018 (Nordicity &
    Miller, 2015, para. 202, Table 18, p. 76); I call this the report’s BYOP uptake. Distributors pass “75%
    of their retail revenue loss to Canadian programming services”, having attributed 86% of that loss
    to Canadian services (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. 206–207, p. 77). By 2020, 10% of the large
    integrated companies’ Category A and B channels close, and 25% of the independents’ (Nordicity &
    Miller, 2015, paras. 232–233, p. 85).
         By 2020 the reform path runs $970 million (23%) a year below the baseline for the revenue of spe-
    cialty and pay services (“channels”, for short, below) and $858 million (9%) below it for distributors’
    revenue (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. xxvi–xxvii). Canadian programming expenditure (CPE),
    which in the report includes distributors’ contributions as well as programming services’ spending,
    runs $399 million below.
    2.1 What the committee heard
    On 12 April 2016 Ian Morrison of Friends of Canadian Broadcasting, accompanied by Miller, put the
    report to the Commons Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage. Its key findings, he said, were
    that “by 2020, some 15,130 media jobs will be lost”, along with “a $400-million drop in Canadian
    program expenditures” that was “18% of what now exists”, “all of this as the direct result of Let’s Talk
    TV regulatory changes”. He added that the authors had “advanced proposals to reduce the negative
    impact of the CRTC’s decisions by as much as 75%” (House of Commons Standing Committee on
    Canadian Heritage, 2016, time marks 0900–0905).
         Two of these phrases depart from the report. The 15,130 FTEs are economy-wide, and 8,300 of
    them are spin-off employment in other sectors, so “media jobs” misdescribes them; and the report
    describes its 18% as a share of projected 2020 baseline spending, not of current spending. The $400
    million rounds the report’s $399 million, and “direct result” attributes the losses to the decisions, as
    the report does, though “will be lost” is firmer than the report’s “likely”. The 18% itself is the report’s,
    and its own figures don’t reproduce it. The report’s own baseline figures put 2020 CPE at $3,155
    million, so its $399 million reduction is 12.6% of baseline, not the 18% its text states (Nordicity &
    Miller, 2015, para. 239, Figs. 8, 9, 43). Against the report’s own forecast for 2015, the reduction is
    12.2%, and against what existed when Morrison spoke, the CRTC’s count of 2015 spending ($3,286
    million), it’s 12.1% (CRTC, 2017). None of the 12 shares tested, built from the report’s figures over
    9 denominators and its two impact figures, gives 18% (table A1).
         Responding to a question about the $25 bundle, one month after the entry-level service launched,
    Morrison cited a much lower uptake figure than the report’s: “the best estimates I’ve seen, Mr. Waugh,
    are that about 4% of Canadians will go for it” (House of Commons Standing Committee on Cana-
    dian Heritage, 2016, time marks 0920–0925). He didn’t say how it bore on the job estimate, and
    “4% of Canadians” doesn’t specify a denominator.

3   Comparing outcomes with the report’s reform paths
    The prediction record (forecasters, quantities, paths by year, stated assumptions) was committed be-
    fore any CRTC outcome series was downloaded. I then re-read the report’s two paths from the data
    labels on its charts, checking every value against the totals in its text, and ran a design analysis by fake-
    data simulation before opening any CRTC outcome file (Gelman & Carlin, 2014; Gelman et al.,
    2020). It asked, for each forecast quantity, whether the outcome could separate a world in which the
    report’s impact occurred from one in which it didn’t, given that the baseline itself could be wrong.
3   Comparing outcomes with the report’s reform paths                                                  5


     That last clause is the hard part. In retrospective analysis of regulation, “developing a credible
counterfactual is the most demanding aspect”, and the baselines assumed beforehand “must be re-
visited” to see whether outside factors changed the result (Morgenstern, 2018, p. 286). Here the
counterfactual is the report’s own baseline, itself a forecast of how television would have fared un-
der streaming without the reforms. So the check treats the baseline’s error as unknown and reports
results across a range of assumed scales for it. Kane (2026) varies the baseline period against which
forecasts are judged; here the baseline is the report’s own, and what varies is how wrong it’s assumed
to be.
     With the annual error scale set to the historical volatility of the report’s own 2010–2014 data,
the design analysis classified specialty and pay revenue correctly in about 99% of simulated worlds
and distributors’ revenue in about 86%. CPE, whose historical volatility was 9.1% a year, came out at
63%, so it was set aside in advance as descriptive.
     For each revenue series I estimate a forecast scale k. In logs, log Bt − log Yt = k δt + et , where
Bt is the report’s baseline, Yt the outcome, δt the report’s forecast shortfall (how far the reform path
lies below the baseline) and et the baseline’s error (positive when the baseline overstates what would
have happened without the decisions), modelled as a random walk from 2014. So k = 0 corres-
ponds to the baseline and k = 1 to the reform path, with baseline error in either case. I write k̂ for
the estimate over 2016–2019, the four years before the pandemic, and k u for the value a recalculated
scenario implies. Because the baseline’s error accumulates from year to year, k̂ rests on changes in the
gap between outcome and baseline rather than on its level: for specialty and pay revenue it’s posit-
ive although that revenue stayed above the baseline in every year, because its excess over the baseline
shrank, the direction the reform path predicted. The report’s paths and the CRTC series are both
in nominal dollars, and since k compares them year by year, deflating both by the same price index
leaves it unchanged.
     The 95% interval for k comes from generalized least squares under that model, and it reflects
only the assumed error in the baseline, so its width depends on the annual error scale σ. That scale
is calibrated from an outside comparison, described below, rather than estimated from four years of
outcomes, and the CRTC’s administrative totals are treated as measured without error (appendix A;
inputs in table A2). The interval treats σ as known. Treating it as uncertain would widen the intervals;
in the illustration in appendix A, the distributors’ interval then includes zero.
     k compares the outcome with the report’s two paths; it isn’t an estimate of the decisions’ causal
effect, which would need a better counterfactual than the report’s own baseline. I also construct a
cited-input band for k by scaling the report’s uptake-related components, in every year, by the ratio
of each cited estimate (10% and 35%) to the report’s 15%, which keeps the report’s ramp, and by
multiplying closures by 2.5 in the high case. The band spans the values of k the report’s cited estimates
imply; it isn’t a probability interval. The plan’s verdicts compare the interval with that band; the
verdict “forecast exceeded”, for example, applies only if the interval lies wholly above it.

(Netflix, Inc., 2019). The report forecast 57.0 million for 2018 and Netflix reported 58.5 million,
which gives a Netflix-derived annual scale of 1.3%, below the historical volatility of both revenue

4.2 Uptake: the report’s assumption and the evidence
The report took its 15% from outside estimates, the highest being Oliver Wyman’s finding that “as
many as 35% of subscribers would be willing to switch to a skinny-basic/BYOP option” (Nordicity
& Miller, 2015, para. 201, p. 75). The CRTC counted more than 66,000 subscribers to the entry-
level package five weeks after launch (CRTC, 2016a) and 177,000 by 30 June 2016 (CRTC, 2016b,
para. 12), about 0.6% and 1.6% of the 2016 subscriber total. These count subscribers to the entry-
level package with or without added channels, a different measure from the report’s BYOP uptake,
taken before every channel had to be offered singly as well as in small packages; I found no later CRTC
count. A survey by the Media Technology Monitor, released in August 2017, put adoption of starter
packages at “just over 1 in 10 TV subscribers” and, separately, reported that 29% of subscribers were
adding individual channels or bundles to their packages (Media Technology Monitor, 2017). Surveys
aren’t among the sources the analysis plan accepted for uptake. This one is self-reported, gives no
fieldwork dates, sample size or margin of error, and may not measure the report’s BYOP share, so it’s
reported beside the planned reading. The survey path below reads its “just over 1 in 10” as 10%, a
floor, and assumes that level held from 2017 through 2019.
     Low uptake in 2016 may partly reflect how the package was sold, a possibility the report itself
raised, that distributors might offer unbundling in a way “relatively unattractive from a price-value
equation, so that very few Canadians opt for it” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 78, n. 40, p. 34).
The CRTC’s hearing of September 2016 examined “the practices certain providers have adopted in
offering” it (CRTC, 2016b, para. 5). Uptake in mid-2016 was far below the report’s 2016 assumption;
whether it stayed low is what the survey puts in doubt.

4.4 Distributors’ revenue by subscribers and revenue per subscriber
Splitting distributors’ revenue into subscribers and revenue per subscriber locates their shortfall. The
report’s components act on different factors: unbundling lowers revenue per subscriber and leaves
subscriber numbers alone, while the exemption order adds cord cutting, 1.9% of subscribers a year
against 1.5% in the baseline (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, paras. 204–205, 216, 220, Tables 5, 21).
Rebuilding the report’s exemption-order component from its stated cord cutting and cord shav-
ing reproduces it to within about 1.1% in every year, so the reform scenario’s split can be recovered
(table A5).
     The comparison is of changes from 2014, since in the report’s actual years the CRTC’s sub-
scriber counts sit 1.7% to 2.1% below the report’s and its revenue per subscriber 0.5% to 0.7% above.
It assumes the two series stay comparable after 2014: the report’s subscriber series includes “non-
reporting BDUs” (Fig. 17; BDUs, broadcasting distribution undertakings, is the CRTC’s term for
distributors), the CRTC’s cover broadcast years ending 31 August, and revenue per subscriber here
is total distributor revenue, which is broader than a package price.
     By 2019, measured this way, subscribers had fallen a further 3.9% below the report’s baseline path,
against 1.5% in its reform scenario. Revenue per subscriber was 8.1% below the baseline path, against
7.0% in the reform scenario: it rose only 1.2% in nominal dollars, from $65.25 a month in 2014 to
$66.05 in 2019 (6.8% lower after inflation), while the baseline path rose 10.1%; its underlying price
assumption was inflation plus one point, before an allowance for cord shaving (Nordicity & Miller,
2015, Table 11, paras. 151–152). With uptake held at the June 2016 share, the partial recalculation
gives a per-subscriber shortfall of 2.5%, and on the survey path 5.3%. Of the total revenue shortfall, in
logs and measured from 2014, 67% exceeds the partial recalculation with uptake held at 1.6% and 44%
on the survey path. In nominal terms distributors’ revenue fell 6.3% from 2014 to 2019, as subscribers
fell 7.5% and revenue per subscriber rose 1.2%; against the report’s rising baseline, about 68% of the
shortfall, in logs, is missing growth in revenue per subscriber.

4.5 Sensitivity to the error scale
Both intervals depend on the error scale (figure 2). The estimates don’t move as the assumed scale
increases; the intervals widen in proportion. The specialty and pay interval reaches the cited-input
band when the scale reaches about 4.0% a year, and at the largest calibrated scale, 4.7%, its upper end
is 0.85. The distributors’ interval includes zero from about 3.0%. At the plan’s upper value of 8% a
year, the specialty and pay interval, −1.13 to 1.37, includes k = 1. None of the reported specialty and
pay intervals excludes zero. The verdict labels in table A4 change as the assumed scale crosses these
values while the estimate stays the same (Gelman & Stern, 2006). Several calibrations were added
after the outcome data was opened, so all of them are shown (Gelman & Loken, 2014).
     The pre-stated Netflix comparison gives a scale of 1.3% a year, below both series’ historical volat-
ility, so the historical volatility sets the designated scale. Other defensible readings of the comparison
give more: the 2016 miss gives a Netflix-derived scale of 4.4% a year and a pooled estimate 4.7%. Part of

    4.6 Checking the error model
    A check of the statistical error model, added after the outcome data was opened, finds the specialty
    and pay residual concentrated in its first step. After fitting k, the standardized changes in the baseline’s
    error between observations are correlated and have variances below one. Divided by their standard
    deviations under the fit, the specialty and pay step from 2014 to 2016 is −1.7, and the largest of the
    three later steps is 0.6 in absolute value; the distributors’ steps show no such pattern. A one-time gap
    between the report’s baseline and the CRTC series would produce this, though with four points so
    would a random walk whose later steps happened to be small.
        Part of the specialty and pay gap is already visible in 2015, a year with no forecast impact, in the
    report’s own figure for that year (itself a forecast). The CRTC later counted $4,290 million, 2.5%
    above the report’s $4,186 million, against 0.8% in 2014 and negligible differences in 2012 and 2013,
    when the report’s figures were actuals. Distributors’ revenue shows no such change: the CRTC’s
    counts run between 1.3% and 1.5% below the report’s figures in every year from 2012 to 2015, a
    standing difference between the two series, with no new divergence in 2015. The 2016 and 2020
    editions of the CRTC’s summaries differ on 2016 by 0.1% for specialty and pay services and 0.5%
    for distributors, so revisions between editions can’t account for the gap. Allowing a level offset in
    the error model, which absorbs both, gives k̂ = 0.20 (standard error 0.18) for specialty and pay
    revenue and 1.23 (0.44) for distributors’ revenue, leaving both pre-stated verdicts as they were; the
    distributors’ comparison with uptake held at 1.6% falls to 1.8 standard errors.

5   Payments, closures and staff counts
    5.1 Payments to Canadian channels
    The report also assumed a path for what distributors pay Canadian channels. In its baseline the
    monthly carriage fee per subscriber for Canadian specialty and pay services rises 1.6% from 2015 to
    2019 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 14, p. 67), and under the decisions it falls below that path
    as distributors pass on 75% of the retail loss attributed to Canadian services. What distributors paid
    Canadian channels in affiliation payments rose from $3,014 million in 2015 to $3,125 million in
    2019 while subscribers fell, so per subscriber it rose 10.5%, and the Canadian share of all affiliation
    payments stayed near 87.8% (CRTC, 2019, Table U-T11). After inflation the total fell 3.5% and the
    payment per subscriber rose 2.9%; the comparison with the report’s fee path needs no such adjust-
    ment, since that path is nominal too.
         The report’s carriage fee per subscriber and the CRTC’s affiliation payments divided by sub-
    scribers differ by definition, so their growth is compared, not their levels. With uptake near the
    CRTC’s 2016 share there would have been little loss to pass on, and the comparison would mainly
    show that payments grew faster than the report’s baseline assumed; on the survey path, the partial
    recalculation has fees falling relative to the baseline path, and observed payments instead grew faster
    than it. The aggregates can’t say what fees would have been without the decisions, or why affiliation
    payments per subscriber rose. Some of the payments stay inside the integrated groups, and the report
    itself expected the exemption order’s impact on those groups to be partly offset by their streaming
    and broadband revenue (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 227, p. 83). The CRTC’s wholesale code,
    scheduled to take effect in January 2016 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, p. 73, n. 82), is in both of the
    6   What the revenue evidence establishes and what the testimony omitted                                11


    report’s scenarios (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, p. 72, n. 80), so its fee path already allows for it.
    5.2 Closures
    For the integrated companies, closures came out close to the report: between 8% and 11% of their
    Category A and B channels stopped operating by 2019–2020, against the report’s 10%. For the inde-
    pendents the bounds, 6% to 33%, contain the report’s 25% but are too wide to test it, because in the
    summaries used here channels that continued under an exemption from individual licensing can’t be
    told apart from channels that closed. Both ranges classify Corus, which owns the former Shaw Media
    channels in the 2016 data, as independent; counted as integrated, as the analysis plan also required,
    it gives 6% to 11% for the integrated companies and 7% to 44% for the independents. CPE, read only
    descriptively, stayed at or above the baseline path through 2019.
    5.3 Staff counts
    For two sectors, the CRTC’s staff counts allow a descriptive comparison with the last link, from
    revenue to jobs (CRTC, 2017, 2021). The report gives its forecast changes in direct employment by
    sector against a baseline whose employment path it doesn’t publish, so the comparison is with the
    change from 2015 and with the 2012–2015 trend carried forward, a crude stand-in for that baseline.
    Specialty and pay services’ staff fell from 5,899 in 2015 to 4,403 in 2019, 1,282 below the trend,
    against a forecast shortfall in direct employment of 800 FTEs by 2019. Distributors’ staff stayed near
    its 2012–2015 trend, 396 below it in 2018 and 1,834 above it in 2019, against a forecast shortfall of
    2,910 FTEs by 2019 (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, Table 22, p. 91). The 2019 figure is a one-year jump
    that reverses the next year, which may be a reporting discontinuity.
         Staff counts and revenue don’t move together as the report’s chain implies in either sector: spe-
    cialty and pay services cut staff while their revenue stayed above the baseline, and distributors’ staffing
    stayed near trend while their revenue fell (annual series in table A6). The mismatch can’t test the re-
    port’s employment prediction: staff counts aren’t the report’s modelled FTEs; the distributor series
    is labelled “Total Staff Count (FTE)” in the 2016 edition and “Total Staff Count” in the 2020 edition,
    and the two differ by 302 in 2016; and a straight-line trend is a weak counterfactual. The direction
    of the mismatch holds with the distributors’ 2018 figure in place of 2019.

6   What the revenue evidence establishes and what the testimony omitted
    In the pre-stated comparisons, specialty and pay revenue, on which most of the job figure depends,
    lost less ground against the report’s baseline than any uptake estimate the report cited implies: at the
    designated error scale its interval for k lies below the cited-input band and excludes k = 1, though
    at the plan’s upper error scale it includes it. Distributors’ revenue was consistent with the forecast.
    These comparisons set outcomes against the report’s paths. They don’t estimate the decisions’ effect,
    and they don’t test the total employment forecast.
         Whether the report’s main input or its chain was at fault depends on how uptake developed after
    June 2016, and on how well the available measures match the report’s BYOP uptake. The CRTC’s
    entry-level share in mid-2016 ran far below the report’s 2016 assumption; a 2017 survey, outside the
    plan’s accepted sources, put starter-package adoption near the report’s figure for 2017. The partial re-
    calculations can say only which assumed uptake paths the outcomes are compatible with. Among the
    paths tested, at the designated error scale, only a rise to about 4% by 2019 keeps both series compatible.
6   What the revenue evidence establishes and what the testimony omitted                              12


With uptake held at the 2016 count, the distributors’ estimate lies 2.1 standard errors above its recal-
culated coefficient (their revenue fell further than the recalculation implies), a gap that every altern-
ative calibration brings below two; on the survey path, the recalculated coefficient for specialty and
pay revenue lies 3.7 standard errors above the estimate, since the recalculation projects a fall against
the baseline that didn’t appear. Larger error scales widen the set of compatible paths. The split of
distributors’ revenue shows their shortfall coming through both fewer subscribers and revenue per
subscriber that grew far less than the baseline assumed. If the uptake assumption was the miss, it’s
a quantity error of the kind Harrington et al. (1999, p. ii) found behind errors in regulatory cost
estimates.
     Three other links can be read without assuming later uptake. Affiliation payments per subscriber
grew 10.5% from 2015 to 2019, against 1.6% in the report’s baseline carriage fee, a growth comparison
of two measures that differ by definition. Closures of the integrated companies’ channels came out
close to the report’s 10%; the independents’ range is too wide to test. Staff counts and revenue didn’t
move together as the report’s chain implies, a descriptive mismatch of unlike measures that can’t test
the employment mechanism; production and spin-off employment aren’t checked.
     Judged against the report rather than against outcomes, Morrison’s summary to the committee
departed from the report in three identifiable respects. It described an economy-wide employment
estimate, more than half of it spin-off employment, as “media jobs”; it described the report’s 18%,
which the report’s own figures don’t reproduce, as a share of current spending; and it didn’t connect
the job figure to the report’s uptake assumption, or reconcile it with the 4% estimate he cited in
questioning (a figure that, like the CRTC’s count and the survey, refers to the entry-level package
itself rather than the report’s measure, and whose denominator he didn’t specify). His appearance
also presented the losses as open to policy, citing the authors’ proposals to reduce them, and when a
member raised officials’ more reassuring view he answered, “If it is right, that’s good news” (House
of Commons Standing Committee on Canadian Heritage, 2016, time marks 0915–0920). What
was lost between report and committee was the numerical condition on which the headline rested, a
particular case of the point Manski (2011, p. 1) makes about policy analysis generally, where point
predictions are common and statements of uncertainty rare.
     The report deserves credit for what made this check possible. It stated its assumptions link by
link, published annual paths for both scenarios, cited the range of outside estimates it chose among,
gave the figures behind its headline, and said that “there is no easy way to assign probabilities to the
projections underlying future scenarios” (Nordicity & Miller, 2015, para. 111). It raised the possibil-
ity of low uptake itself. Publishing only the headline would have left these intermediate predictions
unavailable for checking, and the useful lesson for those who commission or hear such forecasts is in
that contrast. Intermediate predictions that public data can test, an uptake share or a payment flow
in the second year, let a forecast be monitored before its end date, provided someone counts them: I
found no official count of uptake after 2016, which is why this paper has to give more than one read-
ing. And a statement of how wrong the baseline could be shows in advance how much a comparison
like this one depends on it.
6.1 Limits of the check
The paper checks one forecast, and nothing here shows that stakeholder forecasts in general are biased;
Simpson (2014) explains why even a run of overestimates needn’t show that. The verdict on specialty
and pay revenue rests on how wrong the report’s baseline is taken to be. I model that error as a
6   What the revenue evidence establishes and what the testimony omitted                                  13


random walk calibrated in several ways, a simple model for stating the uncertainty, and a structural
break in viewing could exceed it. The intervals also treat the error scale as known, and they assume
the forecast impact applies proportionally to the drifted baseline; if it applied in dollars, as in the
design simulation, they’d cover k = 1 less often than 95% (appendix A). The uptake evidence is thin:
two CRTC counts from the spring and summer of 2016 and one self-reported survey from 2017,
which may not measure the report’s BYOP uptake, so the uptake paths here illustrate the possibilities
rather than bound them. The financial summaries used here can’t separate closures of independent
channels from continued operation under exemption, the aggregates can’t identify the pass-through
rate, and staff counts are a different measure from the report’s modelled employment. At its historical
volatility, the design analysis gave CPE too little ability to distinguish the two simulated worlds for
the planned comparison.
     Five findings don’t depend on the model of the baseline’s error: the CRTC’s entry-level share
in mid-2016 fell far short of the report’s 2016 BYOP assumption; distributors’ subscriber numbers
fell faster than in either of the report’s paths, if the two subscriber series stay comparable; affiliation
payments to Canadian channels per subscriber grew faster than the report’s baseline carriage fee; the
CRTC’s count of specialty and pay revenue for 2015, a year with no forecast impact, was already 2.5%
above the report’s figure, against 0.8% in 2014; and the testimony described the report’s figures in two
ways the report doesn’t support and omitted the uptake assumption.

   Design analysis
   Before any CRTC outcome file was opened, I simulated each quantity 20,000 times (seed 20261003)
   in two worlds: without the forecast effect, Yt = Bt e−et , so that yt = et and k = 0; and with it,
   Yt = Bt e−et − It , the forecast impact applied in levels to the drifted baseline. The second gives yt =
   δt + et (k = 1) up to a term of order et It /(Bt − It ). The simulation code writes the drift with the
   opposite sign, Bt eet ; since the errors are symmetric about zero, the two describe the same distribution.
   The extra term matters for coverage, because it scales the error in yt by about Bt /(Bt − It ): applied
   to paths simulated this way, the 95% interval covers k = 1 in 87% of them for specialty and pay
   revenue and 93% for distributors’ revenue, against 95% when the impact applies proportionally, Yt =

   estimate lies about 3.8 simulated standard deviations from k = 1, against 5.0 standard errors. The
   estimation model is the proportional one, and the intervals in the text are valid under it, given σ.
   Each simulated path was assigned to whichever of the report’s two paths gives it the higher Gaussian
   likelihood under Σ. “Classified correctly” is the share of paths assigned to the world that generated
   them; the figures in the text are the smaller of the two worlds’ shares, at the volatility of the report’s
   own 2010–2014 data.
