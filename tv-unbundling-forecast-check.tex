\begin{abstract}
I check a forecast put to a Canadian parliamentary committee: a commissioned study's estimate that new television rules would cost \lttvJobsTotal{} jobs by 2020. Under a plan fixed before collecting outcomes, I compare its intermediate predictions with regulator data for 2016--2019; neither the job total nor the rules' causal effect is tested. At the error scale the plan set for the study's baseline, the channel revenue on which most of the job figure depends lost less ground against that baseline than the study's model implies at any package uptake it cited, and distributors' revenue was consistent with the forecast, though too imprecisely to tell a shortfall half the forecast's size from one twice as large. Under the largest error scales some calibrations give, both verdicts become inconclusive. How far lower uptake reconciles the channel result with the study's model depends on uptake after mid-2016, when the regulator counted \lttvUptakeJuneShare{} of subscribers on the new entry-level package, against the study's \lttvByopSixteen{} taking it with chosen channels; a 2017 survey outside the plan's sources put adoption near the study's figure for that year. The testimony called an economy-wide estimate media jobs and omitted the uptake assumption.
\end{abstract}
